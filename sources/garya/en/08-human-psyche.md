# 08. On Some Peculiarities of the Human Psyche — notes

## Summary
Andrey goes back to the claim from article 04 that reason follows causal chains only in the directions its "wants" push it. As a result human knowledge is very uneven: deep in some directions, shallow or absent in others. The deepest knowledge sits exactly where the technosphere benefits: energy, physics and chemistry, IT, neural networks, quantum computing, nanotech. Even biology (natural neural nets) and philology (machine language synthesis) end up serving it. Andrey admits Oleg might find a field he can't link, since his own knowledge is limited, but he suspects the pattern isn't chance. Instead of trying to map every link, he proposes a doable method: study the links we *could* see but feel an inner resistance to, using the want of curiosity to watch our own emotions. As a test case he asks why people don't simply build the ideal caring society tomorrow, a "worldwide flashmob with meaning", when the logic is only two moves deep. Together they catch the wants that block it: the sense of social justice, greed, laziness, arrogance, distrust, the urge to stand out ("you're not like everyone else" is the better compliment), envy and egoism. Good and bad wants exist in everyone, which is why no class struggle can produce an ideal society. These opposing forces are not good versus evil. They are one natural law, Hegel's unity and struggle of opposites, which drives all change over time. Then comes a psychological experiment. One event (an early human ties a stone to a stick and builds a shelter) is described first in anthropocentric terms (man the creator, pleasant to hear) and then in technosphere-centric terms (man as the primitive tool of a higher form, fed like fish in an aquarium and later thrown on the scrap heap of history). Oleg is horrified, and the itch he feels *is* his anthropocentrism. Andrey then says both readings are equally wrong because both are polarised, and the truth lies near the golden mean, to be discussed next time.

## Key theses
1. Reason explores causal chains only where wants drive it. Knowledge is therefore highly uneven.
2. The deepest human knowledge clusters in fields useful to the technosphere's evolution, and he suspects this is not a coincidence.
3. Method: rather than mapping every link, examine the links we could see but resist. Use curiosity ("information hunger") to observe one's own emotional reactions, and look through that want alone, impartially.
4. The ideal society is two logical steps away, yet people don't build it. The blockers are wants: the sense of social justice, greed, laziness, arrogance, distrust, the urge to stand out, envy, egoism.
5. The "sense of social justice" blocks communism instead of leading to it. The Marxist classics had it upside down because of anthropocentrism.
6. Good and bad wants coexist in every person. People can't be sorted into good and bad, so class struggle can't produce an ideal society, especially since it has no real grounds anyway.
7. "Good" and "evil" are labels for opposite forces acting on our reason. There is one set of natural laws, not two sources of initiative. Hegel's unity and struggle of opposites drives all change over time, and is "the factor of the existence of time as such".
8. Two polarised readings of tool-making, anthropocentric and technosphere-centric, are symmetrical. The emotional resistance felt to the second shows anthropocentrism at work.
9. Both polarised readings are wrong. The objective reading is near the golden mean, and comes next time.
10. **Human knowledge is substantially uneven. The unevenness comes from unbalanced wants, and it leaves people nearly blind in some directions. Anthropocentrism is one of the wants that blinds human reason.**

## Staging and devices
- Opening image: Andrey moves closer to see Oleg's face better and narrows his eyes. The tone is intimate and probing, a "session".
- The picture of a person standing in a space of causal links with the explored ones "highlighted in colour". The original post has an image at this point.
- Oleg's attempts at counterexamples (biology, philology, then "well, for example... mmm") are cut short kindly: "Oleg, don't strain yourself", with a modest admission that Andrey is "only human" too.
- The "worldwide flashmob" thought experiment, playing on 2011's flashmob craze. Oleg names the blockers himself, Socratic style, and Andrey says "we've caught it by the tail!"
- The girl's compliment ("you're not like everyone else" against "you're like everyone else") as an everyday proof of the urge to stand out.
- Physics analogies against the good/evil split: engine thrust against friction, a ball bouncing off the floor.
- Main device: a guided introspection exercise. Two monologues about the same event. The second ends with chickens fed millet on a poultry farm before slaughter, fish fed in an aquarium, and the human tool thrown on "the scrap heap of history". Oleg: "Creepy! A nightmare!" Andrey: "Listen to your feelings." Then he asks whether Oleg said it or his anthropocentrism did.
- Andrey "calms him down": both readings are wrong, truth in the golden mean.
- Oleg admits a big piece of anthropocentrism has been torn out, and Andrey says far more remains.
- Ending: a summary of the day, a promise to look next time into the areas anthropocentrism kept us from seeing, and Oleg stands up to leave.

## The author in the comments
**Method**
- His own note added to the post: the article describes one of the techniques of Raja yoga for exploring one's own consciousness.

**Reading order and Zeland**
- **Objection** (Ra72: "a vivid sample of sophistic relativism". Later: it resembles Zeland's pendulums, so what valuable thing will we see once we drop anthropocentrism?) → **Answer**: now I see why I couldn't get through to you. The blog has to be read strictly in order, carefully, skipping nothing, because it contradicts so many habits of thought that pieces read out of context look absurd. Showing what becomes visible without anthropocentrism is exactly the blog's purpose. Zeland is only distantly related. There is something in common between his pendulums and Garya's interacting dynamic systems, but Garya sees one single shared reality, not many.

**Communism, envy and the urge to stand out**
- **Objection** (Im27th: with self-sufficient people we'd reach communism; you can still tell a girl she's special under communism; people differ physically and in talents; and under communism everyone can do what they love instead of choosing between painting and feeding a family) → **Answer**: why talk about communism in the future tense? What stops everyone becoming self-sufficient today? Whatever stopped people before will stop them later: the psyche. People don't want to be "like everyone", because they already are, and that means being nobody. A person with low self-esteem envies whoever stands out. The Marxist "who was nothing will become everything" promises this and fails, because once everyone is "everything" nobody stands out again, and the envy returns.
- "Money bags" want to be envied and fawned over more than they want money (money is "just paper"). Those who shake fists clutching volumes of Marx are driven by the same wants: envy, acquisitiveness, the urge to show off, plus low self-esteem.
- A Stone Age man who lived "like everyone" and felt as happy as people do now would see any modern "exploited" worker as a Stone Age Rothschild and call him spoiled, and he'd be right.
- Happiness is relative to the needs bar. A homeless man given 5,000 roubles is in ecstasy, and someone used to 500,000 would hang himself on it. People raise the bar as soon as they reach it, so they are always somewhat unhappy. The Marxist method of making everyone happy is wrong at the root.
- Added comment: the Olympics were meant to let people "let off steam" instead of fighting, and wars didn't disappear. Sport is an *indicator* of the wants, not a way to neutralise them. Whoever wants to score a goal in football wants to "score" outside football too.
- You can't build a society free of envy, greed and the urge to rise by means of envy, greed and the urge to rise, which are the main parts of the "sense of social justice". Real, non-utopian communism is possible only without such wants. If people lacked them, communism would already exist.
- Lenin said "by communist upbringing". But where do the educators come from? Did Lenin, who ordered priests shot, have the moral right? People form their own norms from a balance of wants. Press too hard and they ignore the slogans ("Glory to the CPSU"), put on jeans, become hippies, punks, criminal romantics, and society asks "and who are the judges?"
- The urge to stand out finds outlets under any system: football players' pay, science, a Ferrari, black caviar, the Soviet "Volga" car. If there's no outlet, people invent new ones (nose rings, green mohawks, tattoos), or escape into drugs and alcohol. The drunk's "do you respect me?" comes from the same want: to be "everything", not "nobody".
- Paradox: communism's builders plan a material base, abundance, longer life, organ replacement, everything except modifying people's wants.

**A 2017 reply (six years after the post)**
- **Objection** (Korcar: fish and chickens are fed by people, not by plants, and plants don't eat them, so the analogy fails) → **Answer**: counter-question: why ignore that humanity's evolution fits fully into the planet's evolutionary trend? Evolution didn't stop when humans appeared. It went on by the same laws as before. Some people think they *initiate* the laws and "broke the trend" rather than continued it. A follow-up: ants also "feed" aphids, so do ants "run evolution"?

## Voice and personality
- Therapist-teacher mode. He leads Oleg through self-observation and lets him name the blockers himself, then labels them ("caught another one by the tail").
- Turns theory into felt experience. The "listen to your sensations" exercise is a hallmark device for making anthropocentrism felt as an itch.
- Anticipates counterexamples and concedes his own limits, but treats the overall pattern as telling.
- Mirror symmetry again: he writes an equal and opposite monologue to expose a bias, then rejects both in favour of a middle.
- Unifying natural-philosophy instinct: one set of laws, no dualism. Good and evil reduced to opposite forces, and dialectics treated as a physical law that underlies time.
- Draws on Eastern practice (Raja yoga) and Christian moral vocabulary alike, but not on mysticism. He separates himself from Zeland's "many realities".
- Consistent anti-utopian psychology: wants can't be legislated away. Envy and the urge to stand out are constants across all systems and ages.
- Everyday Russian examples and sayings ("что у трезвого на уме…", "ты меня уважаешь?", "а судьи кто?"), Soviet details (Volga cars, CPSU slogans, jeans).
- Still defending the series in 2017, briefly and with counter-questions.
