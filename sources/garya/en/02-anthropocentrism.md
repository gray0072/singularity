# 02. Anthropocentrism — notes

## Summary
The first fireside evening. Andrey defines anthropocentrism as the view that man is the centre of the universe and the purpose of all events, and claims that everyone, scientists included, is an anthropocentrist; people differ only in degree, and he himself only in being aware of it. Oleg's killing of a mosquito shows a value system in which a human life is worth incomparably more than a mosquito's, without grounds. Oleg calls this harmless, and Andrey answers that other forms are not: Giordano Bruno was burned, and both geocentrism and heliocentrism grew from the wish to have the centre of creation "close at hand". Anthropocentrism is learned in infancy through language: the child sorts things into "кто" (who, like a human) and "что" (what, unlike a human), Russian assigns gender to objects as a pagan relic, and we still say the sun "rises". Calling God "who" and nature "what" leads atheists to deny nature any initiative, purpose or mind without proof. Believers and atheists only argue about *how* man sits on the throne of creation; maybe there is no throne. An anthill elder who thinks ants are the smartest beings, and the search for "little green men" instead of mind, show that a higher mind may be undetectable (Solaris). He introduces a systems criterion: a system made of systems is more highly organized, so a country is higher than a human. When Oleg resists, Andrey points out that Oleg questioned the criterion and not his own bias. Phagocytes act on their own initiative inside the body, as soldiers do inside a country; the only reason we refuse to see a country as higher is that it is a "что". The living/non-living split, and even the "observer" of exact science, are anthropocentric as well. The goal of the evening is to develop an "antigen" against this bias. Promised: reason and intellect tomorrow, then cybernetics, sociology, economics, psychology and AI.

## Key theses
1. Everyone is an anthropocentrist; people differ only in degree, and awareness of it is the first step to reducing it.
2. Anthropocentrism is absorbed from infancy through language (who/what, grammatical gender, "the sun rises") and is never fully removed by school.
3. Geocentrism and heliocentrism share one root, the wish to be at the centre. Modern science still tries to put man at the centre by other routes.
4. Believers and atheists both seat man on (or next to) the throne of creation. That nature has no initiative, purpose or mind is an unproven assumption based on its being a "что".
5. A mind higher than ours may be invisible to us, as humans are to ants. SETI looks for "little men", not for mind.
6. Systems criterion: a system composed of systems is more complex and more highly organized than its parts. So society or a country is a higher form of matter than a human.
7. Method of similarity: human/phagocyte maps onto country/human (initiative of the parts, self-sacrifice, nested life cycles). Logic should not work differently at different levels.
8. The living/non-living dichotomy is anthropocentric. Asking whether a (computer) virus is alive is a badly posed question, like asking whether "green" is round or soft.
9. Even exact sciences carry anthropocentric readings, through the concept of the "observer" (dynamical systems, relativity, Heisenberg).
10. **Anthropocentrism is the oldest, largest and most invisible delusion of humanity, and it makes people blind.**

## Staging and devices
- Opening image: Oleg slaps a mosquito on his forehead and inspects its remains in the firelight. The whole first argument grows from this gesture.
- Analogies and examples: a thumbtack under the buttock (would you kill for that?); a neighbour's cow calving (new information that gets nobody burned); Andrey drawing a scheme with a twig in the sand; a toddler learning words; the gender riddle of пельмень/ремень/плетень vs мишень/мигрень/тень; a window as a "hermaphrodite"; choosing the left or the right buttock on the throne of creation; the anthill elder lecturing the young; Lem's Solaris; the ladder atoms → molecules → cells → organisms → society; phagocytes vs soldiers attacking a tank; anaerobic bacteria, viruses, computer viruses; "is green round or soft?"; "separating flies from cutlets"; developing an antigen; dragging anthropocentrism out by its whiskers and shining a lamp in its face (interrogation image).
- Oleg's objections: he holds scientific views; it is harmless and normal to care for people more; Bruno is ancient history; linguistic forms are just poetry; God and nature are hugely different; "show me a higher form of matter"; a country is made and run by people and has no initiative; a computer virus cannot be alive; philosophy is not exact science and physics and mathematics are immune.
- Andrey's answer pattern: Socratic questions, then exposing a double standard. Key move: "the criteria suited you until they contradicted anthropocentrism; then you questioned only the criteria". He repeatedly refuses to state his own position ("what I think doesn't matter yet; what matters is how I reach conclusions").
- Gradation: he starts with what is easy to agree with and escalates.
- Ending: Andrey looks at his watch and stretches, "time for bed", and invites Oleg back tomorrow for reason and intellect. Oleg still does not see why anthropocentrism is so dangerous, and Andrey's parting line is that it blinds people.

## Voice and personality
- Socratic method: short questions that lead the opponent to state the bias himself, then naming the double standard.
- Signature move: watching *which side* of a conflict the opponent is willing to doubt, and pointing out that the bias was never questioned.
- Uniform logic across scales: he sees no reason the reasoning should change between cell, human and country. Nested systems are a core mental model.
- "The question is badly posed" (green: round or soft?) is a recurring way to dissolve dichotomies: alive/not alive, God yes/no, later free will.
- Includes himself in the critique ("I'm one too, just less, because I know it"), which is a humility move that also claims authority.
- Earthy humour and irony: buttocks on the throne, the hermaphrodite window, the ant elder, flies and cutlets. He pretends not to notice Oleg's sarcasm.
- Medical, agricultural and police metaphors: weeds, antigen, interrogation under a lamp.
- He respects logic and science but treats scientists as people trained from childhood into an unnoticed bias. He claims that removing the bias would allow "a great many new discoveries".
- Withholds his own view on purpose, which builds suspense and keeps the focus on method.
