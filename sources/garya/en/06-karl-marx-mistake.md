# 06. Karl Marx's Mistake — notes

## Summary
Andrey says Marx's theory matters a great deal for the singularity, but it has to be cleaned of anthropocentrism first. Marx treated only people (the capitalist and the hired worker) as active parties in production and treated the "means of production" as passive. The rule from article 04 says that all interacting objects must be treated the same way: either all of them carry initiative, or none do. Marx accepts that supply and demand set the price of any commodity, yet he puts human labour in a separate category with a value of its own, and that exception is the anthropocentric slip. Andrey proves the point with a beekeeper: the same spoon-scraping fetches 500 roubles when the bees made honey and 10 roubles for an empty jar when they didn't, so the bees create the value and the beekeeper appropriates it. The same applies to the mare, the tractor and the machine tool. The technosphere is the real source of surplus value, and the capitalist and the worker share it between them. The worker isn't robbed. He comes voluntarily, because next to the machine he gets far more than his bare hands could produce (the excavator operator's own share is 1/168). The proof that profit doesn't come from exploiting people is that capitalists try to hire fewer people and more machines, which leads all the way to the "lights-out" factory. In return the technosphere gets the chance to exist and to develop. That point is promised for the next evening, together with the true causes of the global crisis.

## Key theses
1. Marx's mistake is anthropocentric. He made people the only initiators and treated the technosphere as a passive instrument.
2. The price of labour, like the price of honey, is set by supply and demand. Labour has no special "objective" value.
3. Bees, horses, machine tools and robots create value. What a producer is made of doesn't matter.
4. "Exploitation" in the technical sense (operating a machine) is the same as Marx's exploitation, meaning the appropriation of someone else's labour. The thing being exploited is the machine.
5. The worker comes voluntarily, to get more than he could make alone, not to hand anything over. Feeling that there is "no other way out" is a mistake: subsistence without means of production is possible, just uncomfortable. Cooperatives are also allowed under capitalism.
6. Capitalists cut staff and add machines, all the way to lights-out factories. So profit grows as human labour shrinks, which contradicts Marx.
7. The technosphere receives no wages. It gets the chance to be (to live) and to develop instead. This is developed later.
8. Splitting matter into "living" and "non-living" is contrived and anthropocentric.
9. **The technosphere is the source of added (surplus) value.** (This line is in bold in the original. An italic paragraph stating Marx's mistake explicitly was added later at the bottom of the article, after the first readers didn't get it.)

**Why capitalists automate: the arithmetic duel with pine**
- **Objection** (pine: automation raises costs, and capitalists cut staff only because of competition and limited markets or resources) → **Answer**: Profit = Revenue − Costs, and wages are a cost. If each worker were a source of profit, profit would equal workers × productivity × base margin, and capitalists would hire as many as possible. In fact they fire. He calls this "second-grade maths", gets visibly angry, and later apologises for the emotion.
- Closed-village model: 100 people, one bakery, everyone buys bread with their wages. The owner replaces 98 workers with one machine, still bakes 1,000 loaves and can sell only 20. The other 980 loaves are the "overproduction crisis" and the 98 jobless are its "unemployment". The crisis comes from people being pushed out by machines, not from exploitation. Elsewhere, a kneading machine in a bakery with no competitors makes the same point: the machine itself "forced" the layoffs, because the owner's wants accepted its offer.
- Tobacco-factory calculation, his showpiece. pine's numbers imply absurd margins, so he uses ~11%, which matches the 9–12% industry rating. A worker alone could make 60 packs a month (3,000 roubles). At the factory he earns 30,000. The machine creates 32,000 of the 35,000 added value (91.4%), and the capitalist keeps 15.6% of the machine's share while the worker gets 84.4%, "and still grumbles that he's exploited". With full automation the capitalist's profit rises 7-fold and the margin goes from 11% to 233%, which explains why automation happens at all.
- Marx's "necessary product" under the same numbers: why would automation change by 3 times the number of cigarettes needed to "reproduce the worker's physical and spiritual abilities"? He mocks the notion that a smoke is "reproduction" for proletarians but not for capitalists. He ends with a jab that a shelf of Marx's books falling on your head could kill you.
- Margin equalisation: capital flows like water between communicating vessels, so cross-industry margins stay around 10–20% and nobody finds 90%.
- His three numbered errors of Marx (to pine): (1) labour's price is set differently from everything else's; (2) the means of production don't create value; (3) overproduction comes from unfair distribution. His version of (3): creating value and distributing it are unbalanced. People and the technosphere create value together, but only human labour is paid, so the technosphere's added value hits the market with nobody to pay for it. "Marx didn't see it, and neither can you while anthropocentrism holds your mind in its claws."
- To Ra72's "the service contractor's workers are exploited": treat it as outside costs (even 10,000 for servicing still leaves profit up 5-fold). The contractor may be a sole trader or sit in a socialist country. Don't mix up different firms' profits. He refuses to keep arguing with people who "multiply entities without need".
- He rejects the claim that this is "speculation": speculation is buying and reselling the same thing, while production turns low use-value materials into higher use-value goods, and the robot adds that value.

**Ricardo, Böhm-Bawerk and the end of economics (with Proverkanad)**
- **Objection** (Proverkanad: equal capitals earn equal profits whatever their mix of machines and labour, so how can machines create value?) → **Answer**: Ricardo is "right globally and wrong locally". In the long run and across the market, price tracks average human labour. But a firm that cuts human labour faster than the market earns a temporary "wave of super-profit", because customers still pay for human labour it no longer uses. Workers also flow between sectors, so a man-hour costs roughly the same everywhere. Higher pay for experts repays their past training costs.
- Example from his own past trade: hand solderers replaced by soldering robots. One million become surplus, and only 10,000 are needed in repair shops, so their wage collapses until people retrain.
- New idea stated only here: in the long run the economy pays only for human labour. **When the human share of labour nears zero, economics loses its meaning.** Making a candy wrapper will take as much labour as building a skyscraper, and labour as the measure of value will vanish.
- He works through Ricardo's comparative advantage with Ivanov and Petrov (boots against potatoes, numbers he says he solved as a student at the Academy of National Economy, АНХ). Exchange happens because *both* gain and because their labour costs *differ*, so there is no "homogeneous congealed labour". Marx's socially necessary labour time is like the "average temperature in a hospital".
- Marx thought too statically. He didn't see that in a fast-moving economy the money is made on local departures from the average, and that these departures are deliberate, not random. Those departures drive the evolutionary trend and in the end the global evolutionary crisis.
- He reads Böhm-Bawerk's critique and cheers it on ("Bull's-eye!", "Exactly!"): volume III of *Capital* contradicts volume I. Prices of new CPUs fall as output grows, which has nothing to do with labour content. Hot new products sell far above cost because of demand alone.
- Even a deliberate mass refusal of technology would not save humanity. A few people unable to resist the freebie and the high margins would keep the technosphere growing. Less-automated producers go bankrupt, which is how Africa lost its farming to high-tech imports (see article 09).
- **Objection** (Proverkanad's 70/30 versus 80/20 example) → **Answer**: there profit stays the same, but only because the capitalist can't count. Real projects go through a business plan with risks such as a workers' revolt. Automation alone doesn't guarantee efficiency (Goldratt), but inefficient automation creates unemployment as well. **Unemployment therefore grows faster than the average efficiency gains from technical progress.**
- The shoemaker ladder: 100 hand-stitchers become 20–30 with sewing machines, and an industrial machine 300 times faster would go bankrupt idle. Mechanisation pays off step by step. The 80 who lost their jobs earned more in total than what now goes to machine makers and repairers, so not all of them find hi-tech jobs, and there is no sector more hi-tech than hi-tech to escape to.
- On riot risk: if a firm replaces its own workers with robots, they may smash the "exploiter". If an unmanned plant is built from scratch, it displaces people indirectly by bankrupting competitors. "People settle scores with whoever is at hand, not with whoever is really to blame."
- On alneo's beekeeper/pimp joke: with real bees the beekeeper takes everything and leaves the bees the minimum, but with technogenic "bees" the gains are of different kinds, and the machine bees may come out ahead. It is symbiosis, not pimping (see article 11).
