# 15. Roger Penrose's counterarguments — notes

## Summary
Oleg has been away reading Roger Penrose's "Shadows of the Mind" and brings it to the fire as a refutation of everything Andrey has said; Andrey has read it too. He calls Penrose a great and honest scientist who still could not escape anthropocentrism, and goes through the arguments one by one. He accepts Gödel's theorem and that a formal system cannot go beyond itself, but says AI systems don't need to: neither AI nor humans are the *source* of mind, only its *carriers*; the source is nature. On Turing's halting problem he answers as a programmer: designed algorithms are protected by iteration limits and hardware timers; "objectively non-computable" results (1/3 as an endless decimal) are computable for every practical case, and for practical value timeliness counts as much as precision. Judging intelligence by the ability to *halt* is odd: the human mind never stops, and its essence is not solving tasks someone else set but setting its own tasks, driven by "wants". A real computer is less than a Turing machine (finite memory) but also more: hardware interrupts and timers let the outside world, nature, take part in control; external initiative cannot be replaced by an extra tape, any more than a girl can sneak up on herself and pull her own braid. Penrose's belief in free will, in "genuinely random" processes and in absolute mathematical truths is, for Andrey, anthropocentrism that puts man in God's place and then proves a machine can't become God. Intelligence lies in avoiding complexity, not swallowing it; quality criteria for mind lie outside, in natural selection; AI is created by nature through human hands, by transferring the principle of images that live, compete and die in memory. Understanding is the recognition of an image; absolute truth is open to neither humans nor machines. At the end Andrey admits doubt: by his own concept no human should have been able to understand what he understood, and he cannot see why he did. There are no conclusions this time except that Penrose did not convince him.

## Key theses
1. Gödel's incompleteness theorem is accepted: mathematical knowledge is partly axiomatic; completeness and consistency cannot both be proven from inside a formal system.
2. Humans and AI systems are carriers of mind, not its source; the source of mind is nature (a book is not the source of the thoughts in it).
3. The halting problem matters only for algorithms that appear "out of nowhere"; properly designed algorithms are guarded against looping (iteration caps with status checks, hardware timers).
4. Practical computability differs from abstract computability: for every concrete task the acceptable error is known, so "objectively non-computable" results are computable in practice; Penrose and Andrey are both right on their own levels.
5. Value of a result has other criteria besides precision, above all timeliness (the machine gun vs the sniper rifle).
6. The essence of reason is posing tasks (setting goals), not solving given ones; goals come from "wants"; AI systems already set tasks for themselves.
7. Humans can't solve every problem either; mastering mathematical induction is learned, and an algorithm given the method can use it; new knowledge arises in AI the same way as in people — self-learning, synthesis of images, generalisation.
8. A computer is not a Turing machine: interaction with the environment (interrupts, timer — "His Majesty Time, Chronos") brings in external initiative that no extra tape can simulate.
9. Randomness means lack of information; it exists only for local systems; belief in "genuine" randomness is the flip side of belief in human free will — both are anthropocentric.
10. A Turing machine cannot be intelligent because it tries to hold the whole universe and its selection criteria inside itself; an intelligent being must be governed by nature, not be the sole source of initiative.
11. Penrose effectively proved only that a carrier of mind cannot be God; he equated man with God, then showed AI cannot reach God.
12. Intelligence shows in bypassing complexity with simpler means; the criteria of a mind's quality lie outside it, in the laws of nature that decide who survives; error is normal for humans and for AI.
13. AI is created by nature through human hands; people only need to transfer nature's principles: recognising, storing, synthesising images and managing their viability. Images are themselves a form of life competing for scarce memory and may "move to a new platform".
14. Understanding is the perception of an image; passing it on means evoking the image in another mind; absolute truth is accessible only to nature, approachable asymptotically by humans and AI alike.
15. The weakest point of the whole concept, by the author's own admission: how could a human understand what, by the concept, no human should understand?
16. **Closing: no conclusions this time — "we only gave arguments for and against"; the single conclusion is that Penrose did not convince him.**

## Staging and devices
- Opening reversal: Andrey greets Oleg after a long absence; Oleg, squinting slyly, hands over Penrose's book as the killer of Andrey's whole concept; Andrey glances at the cover — he has read it.
- Respectful framing of the opponent: Penrose is called great, very smart, objective, with "quality arguments" — and nonetheless anthropocentric.
- The evening is a page-by-page review: Oleg reads out Penrose's arguments, Andrey answers, citing specific pages and chapters of the Russian edition (pp. 166, 216, 235, 243, 245, 250, 319–320, 345, 508, 509, 537, 567, 574, 582; chapters 3.4, 3.5, 3.10, 3.18–3.21, 3.23).
- Everyday analogies: dividing 1 kg of flour into three cake layers with a scale accurate to 10 g; the proverb that a spoon is good at dinnertime; machine gun vs sniper rifle; a person in the electric chair unable to solve "how to survive"; paving Red Square with complex tiles (fill the gaps, break tiles, choose simpler tiles, use asphalt); printing an astronomically large number of ones compared with banging one's head on a wall two quadrillion times.
- Programmer's devices: loop counters, timer interrupts, typing in a word processor (the algorithm formats, the meaning comes from outside).
- Central parable: a boy sneaks up and pulls a girl's braid; she asks "why did you do that?" Then the thought experiment of the girl sneaking up on herself — showing that external initiative can't be replaced by internal.
- "Autism" metaphor for the Turing machine.
- News stories as empirical counter-evidence: robots inventing their own place-name language; the robot scientist "Adam" making a discovery ("machine understanding of the result"); a "starfish" robot creating its own algorithms.
- Feynman's line, quoted by Penrose, "don't listen to what I say, listen to what I mean", turned against Penrose.
- Oleg's objections escalate: computability is enough of a criterion; Penrose speaks of *objective* non-computability; existence problems over the natural numbers; induction; non-deterministic and probabilistic Turing machines; "these are just pretty words"; "I believe Penrose more"; "people are not gods, so they can't create something superior". Andrey answers: "I try not to believe but to weigh pros and cons."
- Light self-irony: Oleg admits he may be an anthropocentrist; Andrey smiles and says he is one too — everyone is.
- Twist ending: Oleg asks whether he is fully sure; Andrey hesitates and confesses the unexplained point of his concept; Oleg "livens up". No formal summary; "time for bed", "good night".
- Nothing explicitly promised for the next evening.

### Qualia and who explains consciousness
- **Objection** (hitthelimit): the closing confession is just an absolutisation of the subject's phenomenology; "understanding" is a qualia-type epiphenomenon; nature has no questions or answers; Penrose is weak; read only authors who first explain the principle of consciousness. → **Answer**: curious — can you be specific? When hitthelimit explains his "level of self-reference cut-off" and names Rorty, Garya replies that he only wanted the names :). He notes that Penrose admits in part one not understanding qualia and in part two suggests a quantum nature. His own view: in a neural-network model of mind, qualia are tied to recognising and synthesising images that reflect reality, or abstractions in a hierarchy of meanings that still ultimately reflect reality; an image's resemblance to what it denotes has objective causes, and those causes make subjects intelligent — so source and carrier of mind are different things. He asks for more interpretations of qualia.

### Artificial vs natural; what the blog warns about (Witewut, September 2013)
- **Objection**: artificial and natural objects can't be separated (anthills, nests, coral reefs, a long fingernail); hence no antagonism, and opposing the technosphere to nature is contrived; dinosaurs too changed the atmosphere. → **Answer**: he said the same himself in article 4 and put it in its summary (point 3); this article shows that AI creation, which people see as their own initiative, is also the work of natural laws operating since before humans. The antagonism is between what "was" and what "will be": dinosaurs are gone, and the blog is a warning that humanity may follow them — not in an astronomical future but within the present generation. There is no absolute antagonism, only the dialectical unity and struggle of opposites. One can view the technosphere as humanity's natural habitat, or humanity as the natural habitat *and breeding ground* of technogenic devices; the first is anthropocentric, the second technocentric, and objectively they are equivalent (end of article 3). He firmly advises against reading the blog "from the end": most readers then object with arguments already dealt with in earlier articles.
- Witewut answers that he read it several times and that the warning itself is programmed by nature; no further reply from the author.

## Voice and personality
- Courteous toward a famous opponent — praise first, then a methodical dismantling, with exact page citations: a reviewer's thoroughness.
- Argues as a practising programmer and engineer: loop guards, timers, interrupts, tolerances; "practice is the criterion of truth" is a refrain.
- Habitual move: grant the opponent's formal result, then show it addresses a different question (abstract vs practical computability; "both of us are right").
- Reframes the question to expose anthropocentrism: "computable by WHOM? knowable by WHOM?"; "spherical horse in a vacuum".
- Homely images and proverbs (flour and cake layers, the spoon at dinner, the braid-pulling boy, Red Square tiles) carry the abstract points.
- Strongly anti-free-will and anti-randomness; nature as the single steering power; humans are not gods and need not be infallible.
- Values doubt explicitly: even the greatest obviousness should leave a shadow of doubt; he says he would like someone to convince him.
- Turns his own theory against himself at the end — a self-reference problem he admits openly and leaves unresolved ("I don't believe in accidents; there must be causes, but I don't see them").
- In comments: brief, friendly, slightly ironic; insists on reading the series in order; restates the blog's mission as a warning.
