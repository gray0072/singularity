# Why Firms Die and Cities Do Not

Andrey was splitting a log into kindling. Oleg came with the notebook already open.

— I wrote it down, — he said instead of a greeting. — Before the phone.

— And?

— A tram, somebody's dog, and my late foreman demanding the quarterly report. Nobody rang. The foreman least of all, thank God.

— The first entry in the expense column.

— Now your homework. — Oleg turned a page. — A mouse. The heart does about six hundred beats a minute, I looked it up. It lives two or three years, if the cat allows. Six hundred a minute is three hundred and fifteen million a year. In two and a half years, eight hundred million.

— And the elephant?

— Thirty beats a minute. It lives sixty or seventy years. Sixteen million a year; in sixty-five years, a billion. — He looked up. — Eight hundred million and a billion. Twenty-five grams and five tonnes. I counted twice, I thought I had dropped a nought.

— Two hundred thousand times heavier, and the same billion.

— Then I counted myself.

— Well?

— Seventy a minute, sixty-two years. Two billion three hundred million. By the mouse's reckoning I ran out at twenty-seven.

— We are an exception, and the bats with us: a bat the weight of a mouse can live thirty years. Most of the furry ones get their billion, give or take a factor of two.

— So for thirty-five years I've been living on credit.

— Nobody has asked for it back so far.

![Chart: the number of heartbeats in a lifetime, by Oleg's count. A mouse of 25 grams, 600 beats a minute for two and a half years: about 0.8 billion. An elephant of five tonnes, 30 beats a minute for 65 years: about one billion. Oleg himself, 70 beats a minute for 62 years: about 2.3 billion and still counting](../images/same-billion-heartbeats.svg)

— Fine. And what has the mouse to do with my town?

— We'll get there by way of the firewood. — Andrey held up a split billet. — Which burns longer: a log, or the same log split into kindling?

— The log. It smoulders till morning. Kindling is gone in ten minutes.

— The same wood, the same weight.

— The fire gets at kindling from all sides. There is more surface.

— So a kilogram of small things has more surface than a kilogram of one big thing. Now, a mouse and an elephant are both warm. Where does the heat go?

— Out through the skin.

— Which of them has more skin per kilogram?

— The mouse. — Oleg grinned. — The mouse is kindling, the elephant is a log.

— So to stay warm, a gram of mouse has to burn faster than a gram of elephant. A German physiologist, Rubner, said so in 1883, after measuring large dogs and small ones.

— Sensible. Where is the catch?

— In the numbers. By the skin, an elephant should need about three and a half thousand times more food than a mouse. It needs about ten thousand times more.

— More than the skin lets out?

— In 1932 a Swiss, [Max Kleiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Kleiber), who studied the feeding of cattle in California, put on one sheet everything that had been measured carefully, from a dove and a rat to a steer. The points lay on one line, and the line went steeper than the skin allows. [His rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleiber%27s_law) in round figures: an animal ten thousand times heavier needs not ten thousand times more energy, and not the five hundred that the skin would give, but a thousand. The mouse and the elephant were put at the ends of his line by others, a few years later.

— So a gram of elephant spends...

— Twenty times less than a gram of mouse. Remember [the watts per kilogram](35-pot-on-the-stove.md)? You shone at one and a half. A mouse gives about eight, an elephant under a half.

— The elephant's cell is twenty times lazier.

— Thriftier. And twenty times slower in everything. Look at your own page: the heart is twenty times slower, the life is twenty-odd times longer.

— So the billion is no miracle. Twenty divided by twenty.

— One clock, run at different speeds. An elephant is a mouse played back slowly.

— But why not by the skin?

— For sixty-five years nobody had a convincing answer. In 1997 [Geoffrey West](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_West), a physicist from Los Alamos who had spent his life on elementary particles, and two biologists, Brown and Enquist, [proposed](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.276.5309.122) to stop looking at the skin. An animal is not a bag. It is a network of pipes: one pipe leaves the heart, forks, forks again, down to the capillaries. And the capillary of a mouse is the same as the capillary of an elephant.

— The same?

— A cell is the same size in both, and so is the last pipe that feeds it. Is the tap in your kitchen smaller than a tap in a twenty-storey block?

— The same tap. The riser is different.

— They set three conditions. The network reaches every cell. The last pipes are the same in everybody. The heart spends as little as it can on pumping. Out of the three came three quarters, Kleiber's line. In a larger animal each capillary has to serve more cells, so each cell gets less and lives more slowly.

— Came out, or was fitted?

— They still argue. Whether it is exactly three quarters, whether small animals sit nearer to the skin's two thirds, whether the pipes are the reason at all: biologists have not agreed. That a gram of elephant lives many times more slowly than a gram of mouse, nobody disputes.

— All right. An elephant is a slow mouse. And?

— Why did you stop growing?

— At seventeen? It was time. Genes.

— A puppy eats. Part of the food goes to keep up what is already built, and the rest goes into building. The food arrives through the pipes, and the pipes, as we have seen, lag behind the weight. The upkeep doesn't lag: every cell asks for its own.

— Income grows more slowly than expenses.

— While the animal is small, income is well above expenses, and the difference goes into growth. The bigger it gets, the smaller the difference. Where the two meet...

— Growth stops. — Oleg frowned. — Wait. That is your curve. Investment, fast growth, slow growth, the ceiling. [The life cycle](10-life-cycle-and-death.md).

— In 2011 I named ceilings that come from outside: the edge of the market, the food, the height to which blood can be lifted. This one grows from inside. The profit per turnover shrinks with every kilogram gained, and nobody interferes. In 2001 the same three [laid on one curve](https://doi.org/10.1038/35098076) the growth of a guppy, a hen and a cow: stretch each by its own weight and its own time, and they coincide.

— One curve for the fish and for the cow.

— And for whatever grows more slowly than its own size. Remember that phrase.

— Remembered. Now the town.

— Now the town. What is a town?

— People.

— The ones who lived there when you left school?

— Half have left, like me. A good many are in the cemetery. New ones came, from the villages first, then from further away.

— The houses?

— Our barracks were pulled down long ago. Five-storey blocks went up, and now there are towers.

— The plant?

— Closed.

— And Genka says "our town", and you know what he means.

— The streets are where they were. The station. The market is on the same square as in my grandmother's day. The bridge.

— So what stands is not what flows. People pass through a town as water passes through the cells in the pot.

— [The whirl in the soup](35-pot-on-the-stove.md) again.

— And as atoms pass through you. Little is left of those you were made of on the day you left school, and nobody says that Oleg did not survive.

— So a town is what remains when the people change.

— And when the plants change. How old is the town?

— Three hundred and something.

— And the plant?

— Built before the war, shut in 2007. Seventy years.

— How many works, workshops and trading houses has the town outlived in three hundred years?

— Who counted them? Hundreds.

— After the animals West asked the next question: is a city an animal? It has pipes too: water, sewers, roads, cables. If it is an animal, a large city must live more slowly than a small one. Does it?

— Ha. In Moscow they run up a moving escalator. At home people stop in the middle of the crossing to talk.

— In 1976 two psychologists [went out with a stopwatch](https://doi.org/10.1038/259557a0) and timed passers-by over a measured stretch of pavement in fifteen towns, from a village to a city of millions. The larger the town, the faster its people walk.

— An elephant with a mouse's pulse.

— In 2007 West, [Luís Bettencourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lu%C3%ADs_M._A._Bettencourt) and three colleagues [published the count](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0610172104) for the cities of the United States, Germany and China. The pipes first. Petrol stations, the length of cables, the area of roads: a city twice as large needs not twice as much but about one and three-quarter times.

— As in the animal. A saving.

— About an eighth less per inhabitant with every doubling. Next: housing, jobs, water in the home.

— Twice as much. A man needs one bed, whatever the city.

— Exactly twice. Now wages, patents, people employed in research.

— Twice... No. You wouldn't have asked.

— Two and three tenths. Every doubling adds about fifteen percent per inhabitant.

— For size alone? The same people, and a seventh richer because there are more of them around?

— And not only richer. Serious crime: the same fifteen percent. New cases of AIDS: the same.

— Wages, patents and burglaries in one basket.

— One rule for the good and the bad alike: for everything that happens between one person and another. Yesterday: thirty in the class, how many pairs?

— Four hundred and thirty-five.

— And of sixty?

— Sixty by fifty-nine, halved. — Oleg wrote. — One thousand seven hundred and seventy. Twice the people, four times the pairs.

— Nobody can meet everybody: there are streets between them, and twenty-four hours in a day. The pipes hold the pairs back. So it is not four times. But it is more than twice. A deal, an idea picked up from a neighbour, a burglary: each needs a second person.

— And what runs in that second network? Not water.

— What do you bring home from a meeting?

— News. A rumour. A thought, on a lucky day. A cold.

— [Images](19-struggle-of-images.md). They breed where heads meet, and a town is where heads meet most often.

![Chart: what changes per inhabitant when a city is twice as large, by the rounded exponents of the 2007 count. Pipes (petrol stations, cables, roads): about 13 percent less. A person's own needs (housing, jobs, water at home): no change. What happens between people (wages, patents, serious crime): about 15 percent more](../images/city-twice-as-large.svg)

— So there are two networks in a town, — said Oleg slowly. — By its pipes it is an animal. And on top of the pipes...

— On top of the pipes runs something that grows faster than its own size. That is the second phrase to remember. The bigger the animal, the slower its heart. The bigger the town, the faster. And the faster heart belongs to another system: not to the pipes but to what runs over them.

— And my plant? Animal or town?

— What did it make?

— Gearboxes. One thing, in forty sizes.

— When it had ten thousand workers, did it sell more per worker than when it had one thousand?

— Less, if anything. Half of the ten thousand carried papers from floor to floor.

— The pipes. By West's count a firm grows as an animal does: quickly while it is young; then the profit begins to lag behind the size, the firm catches up with its market, and from there it grows no faster than the market. That count I know from his book and not from a journal, so weigh it accordingly. What was published in a journal, in 2015, is [the count of deaths](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2015.0120): more than twenty-five thousand companies whose shares were traded in North America between 1950 and 2009.

— And?

— Half of them are gone in about ten years. Whatever the trade.

— Ten years. Ours lasted seventy.

— Yours was lucky. And the strangest thing in that count: the chance of not living through the coming year does not depend on age. A firm of fifty is no safer than a firm of five.

— Then it isn't old age. An old man is likelier to die than a young one.

— Right, and I won't stretch it. A firm does not die as an elephant does, of wear. It dies like a glass in the kitchen: a glass does not age, but sooner or later somebody drops it. The animal part is the growth, which stops. And whatever has stopped growing has only to wait for its turn to be dropped.

— Towns get dropped too. The miners' settlement across the river has stood empty since the pit closed.

— A settlement with one pit is half a firm: it sits on one well, and when the well is used up, it goes. Towns are burnt, flooded and abandoned. But show me one that died of age. Rome is twenty-seven centuries old. What did yours have besides the plant?

— The station, it is a junction. The technical college. The market for the whole district. The river.

— Four wells instead of one. And what do they mean by "died" in that count? For the most part: bought or merged. Few went bankrupt.

— Not buried but swallowed.

— What becomes of the swallowed is a separate evening. What became of the shops of your plant?

— Genka told me. The assembly shop is a warehouse now, for one of those internet shops. The tool room was bought by a small firm: twenty people, they turn parts to order. And the foundry... — Oleg stopped and looked at Andrey with suspicion. — In the foundry there are racks. Servers. Thick walls, the plant's own substation, the river next door for cooling. A watchman and two engineers.

— I didn't say it.

— I am only repeating Genka.

— So the firm died. And what it had built?

— Stands. The substation, the walls, the railway siding, the water intake.

— [The other evening](37-who-exploits-whom.md) we counted: in a bankruptcy the people on both sides of the till lose, and what was built passes to the next owner at a discount.

— So the town is everything that was built, taken together. And the firms are tenants.

— And so are the people. On the forum somebody once put it more sharply than I would: you think towns are where people live? No, they are the habitat of machines.

— Too sharply. A town was built by people, for people.

— It was. Who goes in at the gates of your foundry now?

— Electricity, — said Oleg, after a pause.

He threw a handful of kindling into the fire, and it flared up.

— One thing doesn't fit. An animal stops growing because its income lags behind its size. And a town, where the income runs ahead of the size? Where is its ceiling?

— Let's count. If the gain is proportional to the size, how does a thing grow?

— Percent on percent. A geometric progression. You said it [then](13-singularity-as-process.md): the rate of profit is the exponent.

— It doubles in equal times. Say, every forty years. Now let the gain run ahead of the size: fifteen percent more per head at every doubling.

— Then the next doubling goes quicker.

— By the same eighth. Forty years, then thirty-five, then thirty.

— Twenty-six and a half. Twenty-three. — Oleg wrote them in a column. — Shorter and shorter. Where does it end?

— Add up the column. All of it.

— It has no end.

— The column has none. The sum has: a little over three hundred years.

— Achilles and the tortoise. An infinite number of steps in a finite time.

— An infinite number of doublings in three hundred and ten years. On paper, on that date the town is infinite. Panov wrote the same kind of column for the epochs of life, [we looked at it](29-silence-of-the-universe.md): his shortens two and a half times at a step, this one by an eighth. The sum is finite either way.

— A town can't be infinite.

— It can't. Nothing real flies off to infinity; I drew that for you on the same sheet. Long before the date something runs out: water, land, air. The paper says what comes then: growth stops and turns down. Unless.

— Unless what?

— Unless before the wall somebody invents a thing that moves the wall. A new way to bring water, to get energy, to keep accounts. Then the count starts again, from a new forty years. Only the town is larger by then.

— And larger means faster.

— So the next invention is needed sooner than the last one came. And the one after it sooner still.

— A squirrel in a wheel. And the wheel speeds up by itself.

![Chart on a logarithmic scale: three kinds of growth. What grows more slowly than its own size bends over to a ceiling: an animal, a firm. What grows in step with its size is a straight line, a geometric progression with a doubling every 40 years. What grows faster than its size bends upward: each doubling takes an eighth less time than the one before (40 years, 35, 30, 26 and so on), and the curve goes to infinity at a finite date, about 310 years from the start](../images/three-kinds-of-growth.svg)

— Physicists have a word for the point where a formula goes off to infinity on a finite date. It is printed in that paper, as an ordinary term, like "boiling point". I am not going to read it out.

— Oh no. — Oleg pulled out his phone. — For eight evenings you have been dragging everything to it by the ears. In a paper about petrol stations? I don't believe you.

He found the paper, scrolled, and read out with indignation:

— "...so the singularity is never reached." Well, I'll be...

Andrey said nothing and looked at the woodpile.

— That was a quotation.

— In your voice.

— They wrote it. Physicists. About sewers.

— They wrote it. You said it.

— And you, in eight evenings, not once.

— I read with my mouth shut.

Oleg got up, grumbling, and went off into the dusk. He came back with an armful of birch and dropped it by the log.

— The second time, — he said. — Go on. "Somebody invents." Who?

— In the paper, people. The more of them in one place, the more patents per head.

— And sooner every time. That I don't believe. I learned my trade for fifteen years, school and institute. My great-nephew will be at it till he is thirty: the road to the edge keeps getting longer. And you want inventions more and more often.

— That is a separate evening, and a serious one.

— Don't wriggle. I know what you will say. That there are some who learn in a month and are copied in a minute. They are sitting in my foundry.

— You said that too.

— It is my evening for saying things.

— Then I'll say something else. In 1960 a physicist from Vienna who worked in Illinois, [Heinz von Foerster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinz_von_Foerster), with two colleagues took the number of people on the Earth over two thousand years. It lay on a line of the same kind: the more people, the faster the growth, and faster than by percent. The pairs again: more heads, more inventions, more bread, more heads. They worked out the date on which the line goes off to infinity and put it in the title of [the paper](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.132.3436.1291): "Doomsday: Friday, 13 November, A.D. 2026".

— Twenty twenty-six. — Oleg counted on his fingers. — That was last autumn.

— A Friday.

— And where was I? Sawing up the old apple tree, I think. I noticed nothing.

— Because the line had left that road long before. It kept to it for another ten years or so, and then the growth began to fall behind. The largest addition in one year, ninety-three million people, came in 1989; it has been smaller ever since. [Why](26-why-people-stopped-having-children.md), we have discussed.

— So doomsday was called off.

— Infinity was called off; it always is. That date stood in my table in 2011, a line among the others. What was not called off is the fork from the paper about towns: either the inventions come sooner each time, or the growth stops. The number of heads has made its choice.

— And the rest? Output, wages?

— World output grows by its three percent a year or so. An even geometric progression, and no speeding wheel.

— So your squirrel runs at a steady trot.

— So far it has: every next step was paid for by putting more heads to inventing. That is the same separate evening. Now the heads are running out. Then, by that equation, either the steady trot ends too, or the next steps are made by somebody other than the heads.

— By the tenants of my foundry.

— I believe so. I can't prove it.

— Now my turn, — said Oleg. — Fifteen percent. Where do you draw the edge of a town? Are our dacha plots the town? The settlement beyond the bypass? Move the line, and you have different people, different wages and a different fifteen percent.

— You have hit where they were hit. In 2015 geographers in London [drew the edges](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsif.2014.0745) of the cities of England and Wales in thousands of ways, by density and by who travels where to work, and counted again. Most of the measures came out simply proportional to size. And where they did not, the exponent jumped about with the edge.

— So there is no fifteen percent.

— In England, no steady one.

— And another thing. Perhaps a big city makes nobody richer. The nimble ones simply go there. Who left our class for Moscow? The three best pupils and Genka's brother, who could sell snow in winter.

— In Sweden, where every resident sits in the registers, they [counted exactly that](https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.aav0042) in 2019. About half of the excess is explained by who lives in the big cities and who moves into them.

— Half. And the third thing you'll tell me yourself.

— I will. The line is drawn through many towns in one year. It is a photograph, not a film: one town growing through the years is not obliged to walk along it. And the authors themselves read their equation as a warning, not as a sentence.

— Then what is left of it?

— The sign. I know of no country where large towns walk more slowly than small ones. The animal slows down with size, the town speeds up; whether by fifteen percent or by five decides the date, not the kind. Put a twentieth in place of the eighth and add up your column.

— Forty years times twenty. Eight hundred. — Oleg grunted. — Finite.

— Anything above "proportional" gives a finite sum. And one more thing is left, the one that matters to me. On that sheet in 2011 I drew a curve with a wall on a finite date and said that the real thing is not a point but a stretch of years in which the old law stops working. I came to it from the epochs of life and the count of people. These men came from a mouse, a petrol station and a stopwatch on a pavement, and got the same drawing: a wall on a finite date, and before it either a stop or a change of law.

— Two bell towers, one view.

— Or two pigeons at one feeder. That one is already on your last page.

Oleg opened the last page.

— Came with a closed plant. Came out at the servers in its foundry, brought them in myself, and went for firewood on top of it. Again.

He put an eighth tick beside the seven.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **What grows more slowly than its own size lives at a measured pace: the larger, the slower. It stops growing, and then it dies or is swallowed. Such are an animal and a firm.**
2. **What grows faster than its own size has to speed up: the larger, the faster. On paper it reaches infinity on a finite date; in life, before that date, it either stops or jumps to a new invention, and each next one is needed sooner than the last. Such is the network of meetings that runs over a town's pipes.**
3. **A town is not its people and not its plants but the network through which they flow, as atoms flow through a body. That is why the plant is closed and the town stands. The question is not whether the wheel will keep speeding up, but who will make the next jumps when heads can make them no faster.**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: a gram of elephant lives many times more slowly than a gram of mouse; half of the traded firms are gone in ten years; large towns are richer per head and walk faster, wherever a country was measured in one year. Argued about: whether it is exactly three quarters and whether the pipes are the cause; the fifteen percent, the edge of a town, and how much of the excess the newcomers bring. A hypothesis of its authors: the equation with the wall and the wheel. Mine: that the technosphere is of the second kind, and that the next jumps will not be made by heads.

— Strange, — said Oleg. — The plant is dead, and it doesn't come out gloomy. The town stands.

— The town stands.

— Only I forgot to ask who lives in it.

— You asked. And answered: electricity.

— Is that objective?

— To within the edge of the town.

Oleg closed the notebook and nodded towards the house.

— Genka says the class will meet in the House of Culture. It stands too, would you believe. He is asking who of ours still plays: there were three guitars in the class once, and now there is none. Is yours alive?

— The seven-string. It hangs on the wall.

— Bring it tomorrow. I have a question about it. What is it for at all?

— Tomorrow.

— Then tune it.

— And you write down the dream. Before the phone.

— Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
