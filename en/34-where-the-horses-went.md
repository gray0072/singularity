# Where the Horses Went

The firewood behind the spruce had not been touched since yesterday. Oleg checked that first, and only then sat down on the log and opened the notebook.

— Today it's your turn to slip, — he said. — I've brought you horses.

— I remember. The riding club.

— Here. — Oleg held out his phone. — A club behind the station. An hour in the saddle costs what I earned in a day when I was young. And my grandfather kept a horse to plough with, not to be photographed on. Where did the working animal go?

— What did your grandfather's horse do?

— Everything. It ploughed, carted hay and firewood, took potatoes to the market and my grandmother to the doctor.

— So it had not one job but a bundle of them. And how many such horses were there? Let's take a country where they were counted every year and where nothing got in the way of the count. Ours won't do: two wars went over it, and more besides.

— America, of course.

— America. In 1900 its farms had [about twenty-one million](https://hsus.cambridge.org/HSUSWeb/toc/treeTablePathIdDa983-987.html) horses and mules. I'll say "horses" for short. The herd went on growing and reached its peak in 1918: [26.7 million](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jechis:v:61:y:2001:i:03:p:663-698_03). The country then had a hundred million people in it.

— One horse for every four people.

— On the farms alone. A few million more worked in the towns and the mines. Now guess how many were left on the farms by 1960.

— Half?

— Three million.

Oleg whistled.

![Chart: horses and mules on the farms of the United States, 1900–1960. The herd grows from 21 million to a peak of 26.7 million in 1918 and falls to 3.1 million by 1960; over the same years the number of tractors on farms grows from a quarter of a million in 1920 to more than 4.5 million in 1960](../images/horses-on-farms.svg)

— Minus twenty-three million in forty years. And where did they go?

— What's your guess?

— What is there to guess. To the knacker. Sausage, glue and dog food.

— Everybody thinks so. In 1940, 314 thousand horses and mules [disappeared from American farms](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jechis:v:61:y:2001:i:03:p:663-698_03). Nineteen thousand of them went through the slaughterhouses: six in a hundred. Later the share grew, but even at its highest, around 1950, it was less than half.

— And the rest?

— Died of old age behind the barn. Many farmers bought a tractor and kept the old horse all the same.

— Sentimental people, farmers. — Oleg slapped a mosquito, the first of the year. — But then the arithmetic doesn't work. If nobody killed them, why did the herd shrink?

— How long does a horse live?

— Twenty-five years or so.

— And it works for fifteen or twenty of them. What has to happen every year for a herd of twenty-six million not to shrink?

— Foals have to be born. As many as the old ones that die.

— About a million horse foals and three hundred thousand mule foals a year. In 1931 the Department of Agriculture wrote with alarm that only half that number was being raised. By the middle of the twenties the main breeding states had less than a third of the registered stallions they had had ten years before. And the herd grew old: in 1923 the average horse on a farm was about nine, in 1935 over twelve and a half.

— So the horse wasn't sacked.

— It wasn't sacked and it wasn't slaughtered. It wasn't born.

Oleg was silent for a moment.

— And who decided that?

— Who decides whether a mare is taken to the stallion?

— The owner.

— Did the owners hold a congress and vote to abolish the horse?

— Hardly.

— Each one counted for himself. A foal eats for three years before it can be put in harness. A farmer added up those three years of oats, hay and trouble and compared the sum with the price of a grown horse at the fair. And that price, in real money, more than halved between 1915 and 1925. When a grown horse costs less than it takes to raise a foal, nobody orders the foal.

— But did anyone ask the horse?

— Did anyone ask you when you bought a car?

— Me? — Oleg was taken aback. — Nobody. I worked it out: the bus didn't go to the dacha, and the car did.

— Nobody asked the farmer either. His neighbour with a tractor ploughed more and sold cheaper. He could buy a tractor too, or sell the farm to the neighbour. In 1920 there were a quarter of a million tractors on the farms, by 1929 about a million, by 1960 more than four and a half million. From the middle of the fifties there were more of them than horses. No law was passed, and there was no villain.

— All right. A sad story, but an honest one. — Oleg threw a branch into the fire. — What else did your Americans count?

— The oats. What does a horse run on?

— Oats and hay.

— A grown farm horse needed the harvest of about three acres a year, a little more than a hectare. Multiply.

— Twenty-six million by three... Eighty million acres. That can't be right.

— It's right. In 1915 the feed for horses and mules took [ninety-three million acres](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:cup:jechis:v:61:y:2001:i:03:p:663-698_03) of cropland. The farm animals ate the harvest of twenty-two percent of all the land under crops, and the ones in the towns and mines another five.

— Wait. More than a quarter of all the fields in America grew fuel?

— Fuel for the engine of that time. By 1960 five million acres were enough. The difference is a field the size of Germany, and I'm not counting the pastures.

— That I will retell at the table, — said Oleg and made a note. — And who got the field?

— People and cattle. Bread and meat got cheaper.

— So everybody gained.

— Everybody who stayed.

Oleg looked at the phone again.

— And these, from the riding club? They are alive and well, and not cheap at all.

— In 2023 there were [6.65 million horses](https://horsecouncil.org/economic-impact-study/) in America. Twice as many as at the bottom.

— Doing what?

— Carrying people for pleasure. Riding, sport, shows, racing.

— So the bottom wasn't zero.

— It wasn't. But look who pays now. Your grandfather's horse fed itself and your grandfather too. Today's horse eats the same oats and needs a stable, a blacksmith and a vet, only the plough no longer shares the bill. The whole of it is paid by somebody's wish to sit in a saddle. That is why your hour costs a day's wages.

— What was a tool has become a treat.

— A want. And today's horse lives better than its great-grandmother, by the way. Nobody whips it uphill with a cart of coal. It has a rug for the winter and a doctor of its own.

— Those that exist.

— Yes. There is one for every fifty people. There used to be one for every four.

Oleg closed the notebook and squinted at Andrey.

— I see where this cart is going. "It wasn't sacked, it wasn't born." In autumn you said that, and not about horses. And I've read this comparison before. [Leontief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Leontief), a Nobel laureate who studied in Petrograd, by the way, [said in 1983](https://en.wikiquote.org/wiki/Wassily_Leontief) that the role of humans as the most important factor of production is bound to diminish the way the role of horses did when the tractor came.

— Note that it's you who harnessed people to this cart.

— I did, in order to unharness them at once. Economists answered Leontief long ago: a human is not a horse. First. A human retrains. Machines have been taking work away for two hundred years, and there is more work than ever.

— Good, let's take the first. The steam engine came a hundred years before the tractor. Why didn't it do away with the horse?

— It... — Oleg thought. — I don't know. It should have.

— Between 1840 and 1900 the American herd [grew sixfold](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/2015-06-16/will-humans-go-way-horses). The railway carried freight to the station, and from the station to the door it went by cart. The more rails, the more carts. If horses had had economists, they would have written: the machine doesn't take our work, it adds to it, and here are a hundred years of statistics.

— And they'd have been right.

— For a hundred years. The steam engine took the neighbouring job, the long haul, and the horse's own job grew. Then came a motor that was small, needed no rails and went wherever a horse goes. That one took the horse's own job. It was the same with the [bank tellers](24-last-profession.md) in autumn: the cash machine added branches, and the phone took away the visit to the bank.

— And the retraining?

— The horse retrained as well: from the plough to the saddle. Into the one trade where a motor can't replace it, being a live horse. In autumn we called that the last shield, "made by a human". Under it there turned out to be room for one horse in four, in a country with three times as many people.

— All right. Second. There is a theorem that nobody has refuted: [comparative advantage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comparative_advantage). I read an article where it's explained with a surgeon. The surgeon operates better than his assistant, and lays out the instruments better too. Even so it pays him to operate and to leave the instruments to the assistant. The weaker one always has something left to do.

— I know that article. I argued with it on the forum in 2017, and I asked then: why did we stop riding horses? By that theorem the horse should have kept its place beside the tractor. Perhaps the farmers simply hadn't read Ricardo?

— Don't joke. Where is the hole?

— There is no hole in the theorem. It promises that there will always be a price at which the weaker one is worth hiring. It doesn't promise what that price will be.

Andrey took a stick and drew a level line in the ash, and above it a line that sloped down and crossed it.

— The level one is the feed: oats, hay, the stable. It doesn't get cheaper because a tractor has appeared. The sloping one is what a horse's day of work is worth: no more than the same work costs when a motor does it. An economic historian, Gregory Clark, [put it in two sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Farewell_to_Alms). There was always a wage at which all these horses could have stayed employed. But that wage was so low that it did not pay for their feed.

![Scheme drawn in the ash: a level line is the cost of a horse's feed, a falling line is what a horse's day of work is worth, no more than the same work done by a motor. To the left of the crossing the work pays for the feed and foals are raised; to the right there is still a wage, but it is below the feed, and foals are not raised](../images/wage-below-feed.svg)

— And to the right of the crossing the foal isn't ordered.

— And there is a second thing the author of the surgeon forgot. He compared two options: the surgeon alone, or the surgeon with a weak assistant. There is a third: a second surgeon just as good, at the assistant's pay. With people that doesn't happen. With tractors it does: the second one comes off the same line as the first.

— Hm. — Oleg rubbed his chin. — And what is the oats of a human?

— You tell me.

— Food, a roof... No, wait. A foal eats for three years before the harness. My niece's child would eat for twenty-five, counting the institute. — He fell silent. — That was your [evening about children](26-why-people-stopped-having-children.md). The same sum, only nobody takes anybody to a stallion. People decide it themselves.

— By the same arithmetic. I wouldn't press the likeness further: your niece is not a mare, and she counts more than money.

— Now the third, — said Oleg. — This is the main one, and oats won't get you out of it. The horse didn't own the tractor. It didn't vote and it didn't write laws. People own the machines, and people decide how to divide what the machines make. That is how [the economists answered](https://www.foreignaffairs.com/articles/2015-06-16/will-humans-go-way-horses) Leontief, and I think it's a good answer.

— It's the best there is. And I'll add one to it from my side. The horse was never a customer: nobody made anything in order to sell it to a horse. For a human, things are [still made](25-freebies-for-everyone.md).

— You're helping me?

— I'm counting honestly. Now a question. Did the farmer own his tractor?

— He did.

— In 1935 America had [6.8 million farms](https://www.ers.usda.gov/data-products/ag-and-food-statistics-charting-the-essentials/farming-and-farm-income). Now it has fewer than 1.9 million. The owner of a tractor was pushed out by the owner of a bigger tractor.

— But somebody still owns it. A human.

— Somebody does. So ownership protects the owner, and "people own the machines" breaks into questions: which people, how many of them are left, and why what is owned gathers in fewer and fewer hands. About the vote we spoke in autumn: it weighs something while the voter holds something that is needed. I won't close this tonight, Oleg. Of everything in which a human differs from a horse, ownership is the one thing I can't brush aside, and it deserves more than the tail of an evening.

— Then I'm writing it down. — Oleg opened the notebook. — "Who owns the tractor." And separately: "Why money goes to money."

— Write. Those are two evenings, not one.

— And the forty years from the peak to the bottom? Will you convert them into human years?

— No. Forty years is two working lives of a horse, and that's all the precedent says. I named my dates in autumn, with the odds, and I won't add new ones from a horse.

Oleg leafed back through the notebook, then looked at the fire.

— We've come out there again. At your sing...

He stopped short and glanced towards the spruce.

— At my what? — Andrey asked innocently.

— At your favourite word. I'm not going for firewood two evenings in a row.

— Half a word doesn't count, — Andrey agreed. — The tick does.

Oleg turned to the last page and put a second tick beside the word "again".

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **The horse was not sacked and not slaughtered: people stopped breeding it. Nobody decided that. Each farmer compared the price of a grown horse with the cost of raising a foal. The displacement of a carrier goes through births.**
2. **It takes decades and goes in steps. For a hundred years the steam engine added work for the horse, until a motor came that did the horse's own job. From the peak to the bottom took forty years, two working lives of a horse.**
3. **What remains is not emptiness but a luxury. A tool became a want: today's horse is kept for love and for status by those who can afford it, one for every fifty people where there used to be one for every four.**

— And what is left open?

— Whether ownership and the vote make a human a different case from a horse. It's in your notebook, in two lines.

— Gloomy, — said Oleg. — Even with the rug and the doctor.

— But objective.

Oleg got up, then sat down again.

— One thing nags at me. Nobody asked the horse. Nobody asked the farmer. Nobody decided anything, and yet everything rolls one way: the horse, then the tractor, then a bigger tractor. Who is pushing?

— Is that a question for the notebook?

— It is. "Who is pushing all this."

— Then tomorrow I'll bring a pot.

— What for?

— For soup. You'll see the answer on its surface before it boils.

— Soup instead of an answer. — Oleg stood up. — Fine. But I'm bringing my own spoon. Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
