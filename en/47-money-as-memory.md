# Money as Memory, and What Will Come Instead

Oleg came with the notebook, and before sitting down took two pieces of paper out of it and laid them side by side on the log.

— The dream first. Before the phone. I am at the till in our grocery, paying with red tens, the ones with Lenin. The girl takes them without a word and gives me my change in beer caps. I count the caps, and she has short-changed me. Nobody rang.

— The eighth entry in the expense column.

— And in the morning I went to look, and there it was. In the second volume of Chekhov, where I put it in ninety-one and forgot. — He tapped the red one. — And this is today's. You said to bring a banknote. I brought two, to have something to compare.

— Read today's first. Everything that is written on it.

Oleg tilted the note towards the fire.

— "Ticket of the Bank of Russia. One thousand roubles." Yaroslav the Wise, the one the whole country calls the man with the cake. And in small print along the edge: "Forgery of tickets of the Bank of Russia is prosecuted by law." That is all.

— A ticket to where?

— It doesn't say.

— What does it promise you?

— Nothing. It only threatens.

— And the old one?

— The old one is polite. "Bank tickets are backed by gold, precious metals and other assets of the State Bank." — He raised a finger. — That was money. It had gold behind it.

— Did you ever go to the State Bank for your gold?

— They would have called a doctor.

— So one promised and did not mean it, and the other does not even promise. The English are the most honest. Theirs says: "I promise to pay the bearer on demand the sum of ten pounds." You can take it to the bank and demand.

— And they give you gold?

— They give you another ten pounds.

Oleg snorted.

— Then why did the man with the birch count my thousands twice and put them inside his cap?

— You tell me.

— Because the shop takes them. And the shop because the supplier takes them. The supplier pays the driver, and the driver buys birch. — He stopped. — A circle. Everybody takes it because everybody takes it.

— And the red one? The paper is whole, the print is clear, Lenin is in his place. What broke?

— The circle. In the summer of ninety-three they stopped taking it, and that was that.

— So it is not in the paper. Let's put the paper aside and look for the circle where there is no paper at all. The neighbour across the fence, the one with the roof. What receipt did you take from him?

— None. I remember, he remembers, and half the street knows.

— And when he ploughed your potato patch this spring, what did you pay?

— Nothing. He had my pump all last summer.

— Who owes whom now?

— By my count, he owes me a little. By his, I suspect, the other way round. — Oleg grinned. — We don't sit down to settle.

— So you keep a book.

— In the head. Both of us.

— And the man with the birch?

— He is from the far end. I know him by his cap. — Oleg paused. — With that one, money.

— Remember it. Now the textbook. How did money begin?

— Everybody knows that. First they bartered: I give you an axe, you give me a sheep. It is awkward: I have an axe, and you don't need an axe, you need a pot. So they agreed on one thing that everybody takes.

— That is Adam Smith, 1776: the butcher, the brewer and the baker. It sounds so obvious that for two hundred years nobody checked. Then the ethnographers went looking for a village that lives by barter. In 1985 a Cambridge anthropologist [summed up](https://doi.org/10.2307/2802221) what they had found: no such economy has ever been described, let alone money growing out of it.

— And what do they live by?

— By what you have with the neighbour across the fence. Everybody owes everybody a little, and everybody remembers.

— But barter does exist. — Oleg sat up. — I lived in it. In ninety-six the plant paid us three months' wages in enamel saucepans. Our gearboxes went to a combine works, their combines to some farm, and by the fifth link it came down to saucepans. My wife is still cooking in one.

— Who bartered with whom? People who had known each other since childhood?

— Plants that had never heard of each other. Through middlemen nobody trusted.

— And did they know what money was?

— Of course. There just wasn't any.

— That is exactly where the ethnographers found barter: between strangers, and among people used to money when the money has gone. By 1998, by the surveys of directors, about half of what Russian industry sold was paid for without money. Barter is not what came before money. It is what is left when money leaves.

— All right. Then what came before?

— An island. [Yap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rai_stones), in the Pacific. The money there was stone: discs with a hole in the middle, from the size of a plate to twice a man's height. There was no such stone on the island. It was cut on another one, more than four hundred kilometres away, and brought over open sea on rafts. An American doctor, Furness, spent a few months there in 1903 and wrote [a book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Henry_Furness_III) about it.

— How do you take a thing like that to market?

— You don't. A big stone stood where it stood, leaning against the wall of its former owner, and everybody knew that it was now another man's.

— A village. Everybody remembers.

— And the best stone of one family nobody alive had ever seen. Their forefathers were bringing it home, a storm came up, the raft had to be cut loose, and the stone sank. Those who got back told how big and how fine it was, and that it was lost through no fault of the owner. The island decided that a stone on the bottom is still a stone. When Furness came it had been lying there for two or three generations, and the family was counted among the rich.

— A coin on the sea bottom. And they could pay with it?

— They did.

— Savages, — said Oleg with pleasure.

— Then the Germans bought the islands and wanted the paths repaired. The islanders did not. So an official went round the villages and painted a black cross on the best stones: these now belong to the government. The paths were repaired at once. The official went round again and wiped the crosses off.

— And nothing was carried anywhere.

— Now the test of symmetry. Where is your pension?

— On the card.

— And where is the stone?

— In the bank. A number.

— Was it ever paper?

— No. It comes as a number and it leaves as a number.

— Most of the money in the world has never been paper. And now 1932. The Bank of France is afraid for the dollar and asks the bank in New York to turn its dollars into gold. Not to ship it across the ocean, but to keep it there, as French. The clerks go down to the vault, move the bars into separate drawers and put labels on them: property of France. The newspapers write that America is losing gold, and it adds to the panic in which the banks were shut the next spring. Not a bar left the cellar.

— A black cross. From the other side.

— The economist [Friedman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milton_Friedman), who dug the story up, asked just that: what is the difference?

— So the man with the birch... — Oleg looked at the thousand. — He wants a stone that leans against his own wall. And my numbers are for him a stone on the bottom: everybody says it is there, and nobody has seen it.

— He trusts his own pocket more than somebody else's book. And paper has one more convenience. A number remembers who gave to whom. Paper says only "the bearer".

— And the tax office need not know about the birch.

— I did not say that. Tell me instead: what does the whole island remember about a stone?

— Whose it is.

— And how did it become his?

— He gave something for it. A pig. A boat.

— Then the stone is a mark: this man has given and has not yet received. Now imagine a book in which everything that anybody ever gave to anybody is written down, and everybody can read it. Is the stone needed?

— No. Look in the book.

— In 1998 an economist proved it as a theorem: whatever money can do, such a book can do, and the book can do more. The paper is called ["Money Is Memory"](https://doi.org/10.1006/jeth.1997.2357). Money is the book of the poor: one line torn out of it, to carry about. "The bearer has given, and has not yet received."

— A poor memory. It doesn't say what he gave, or to whom.

— A primitive one, the author says so himself. But it fits into a pocket, and it works between people who will never meet again.

— The hundred and fifty. — Oleg leafed back. — [The evening about the ants](43-why-ants-do-not-strike.md). A head holds a village, and further on come the prostheses. You said then: money is a record that you once gave somebody something. I put it down.

— Then here is the proof that the book came first. When people learned to write, what did they write?

— Prayers. Or the victories of kings.

— The oldest writing we have is from [Uruk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uruk), in the south of Mesopotamia, about fifty-three centuries old. Several thousand clay tablets. More than four in five are accounts: barley, beer, sheep, cloth, who was issued how much. The rest are lists of signs for training scribes. Not a verse, not a prayer, not a king, for the first few hundred years.

— Stock-keepers.

— One tablet says: 29,086 measures of barley, 37 months, and a sign, [Kushim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kushim_(Uruk_period)). It may be the oldest name of a person that has come down to us. Or the name of an office; that is argued about.

— Not a king, in either case. — Oleg was pleased. — A man from the supply department.

— There were tens of thousands of people in Uruk by then.

— More than a hundred and fifty. The book no longer fitted into a head.

— And it moved onto clay. The coin was struck in [Lydia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydia), in the seventh century before our era. Twenty-seven centuries after the tablets.

— As long as from that coin to us.

— The ledger is as much older than the coin as the coin is older than you.

![Timeline from 3500 BC to today: the clay tablets of Uruk with accounts of barley, beer and sheep, about 3300 BC; the first coins, Lydia, about 630 BC; today, a number in a bank's book. Twenty-seven centuries lie between the tablets and the coin, twenty-six between the coin and us](../images/ledger-older-than-coin.svg)

— Half the banknote is read: it is a record. Now take the red one. The record is whole. Why is it worth nothing?

— Nobody honours it.

— What must a man want, to take your paper?

— To get something for it later. From somebody else.

— From a person.

— From who else?

— So the paper holds as long as people need something from one another. Robinson finds a drawer of coins on the wreck, gold and silver. Do you remember what he says to them?

— No.

— "O drug! what art thou good for? One of those knives is worth all this heap." And he tells them to go to the bottom.

— On Yap that would not have hurt them.

— And the next line: "However, upon second thoughts I took it away."

Oleg laughed.

— One of ours.

— On the island the record is whole, and there is nobody to present it to. Then Friday arrives. Two people who need each other badly. Do they start money?

— What for? They see each other every day.

— So there are two conditions. Name them.

— Somebody has to need something from you. — Oleg bent one finger, then another. — And he must not be your own. Neither kin nor from your village. A stranger who needs you.

— In 2017, on the forum, I called money the equivalent of people's need for people. That is your first finger, and I stand by it. The second I did not see then: a need between one's own gets along without money.

— So what is money?

— A record that somebody needed you. You carry it about because he was not your own, and will not remember.

Andrey smoothed the ash and drew the columns.

— Fill it in. Who; do they need one another; are they one another's own, by kinship or by a common book; and what stands between them.

— Robinson alone. No need, so nothing. — Oleg took the stick. — My wife and I. Need: yes. Own: yes. Nothing. If she billed me for forty years of soup, I would be bankrupt. The neighbour across the fence: a book in two heads. The man with the birch: need, and not my own. Money.

— Your plant, inside. Did the assembly shop buy castings from the foundry?

— On paper it did. We had internal cost accounting, every shop with an account of its own.

— Could you refuse the castings and buy them from another foundry? Could the head of the foundry take the takings home?

— No.

— Then it was a book, not money. In 1937 an Englishman, [Coase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Nature_of_the_Firm), asked a child's question: if the market is so good, why are there firms at all? Why does the director give orders, instead of buying every operation from a turner at the gate each morning?

— Because he would spend the whole morning haggling.

— That was the answer. The market costs: to find, to bargain, to check, to sue.

— Half the plant in the gatehouse.

— So wherever people work together for long, they shut the market out and put a foreman and a book inside. A firm is an island with no money on it, in a sea of money. Coase quotes a colleague: like lumps of butter in a pail of buttermilk.

— And the lowest row?

— Your liver and your kidneys. Does the liver sell sugar to the head?

— It issues it. — Oleg thought. — From the store, on a signal.

— All your blood holds about a teaspoon of sugar, and the body keeps that teaspoon within narrow limits day and night. The liver holds a reserve of about a hundred grams for everybody, and has never charged interest. The strictest of accounts, and not a kopeck of money.

— Because they are kin of one. — Oleg put the stick down and looked at the table. — Stop. I can see which row you want next, and I'll write it myself, to save you going round. Those ones. Copies: closer than sisters, letter for letter. One book for all, and nothing forgotten. Need without end, and all their own. So inside, no money. I said it, not you.

— I did not open my mouth.

— You didn't have to. And this is where I've got you. — He took out his phone. — It is the other way round. They are being given wallets. In 2025 both of the big card networks published rules by which a program pays for its owner. My granddaughter told hers to find tickets, and it found them and paid while she slept. And look: the rules of the web have had [a code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HTTP_402) since the nineties, the four hundred and second, "payment required". Thirty years it stood there with the note "reserved for future use". Now it is in use: a program knocks at a page, the page answers "four hundred and two, the price is such-and-such", the program pays a fraction of a cent and goes in. No human looked. Money has begun to pass among those ones with no person in between.

— In 2017 a reader told me that a robot cannot be paid, by definition: whatever the owner earns, he will pay to people. Now you tell me that robots are being paid. Both times as an objection.

— Well?

— Whose money is in the wallet?

— My granddaughter's. Her father's, that is.

— And whose want are the tickets?

— Hers.

— And the program at the other end, the one that sold them?

— The airline's.

— Two owners. Not kin. Each needs something from the other, and they will never meet. Which row?

— The man with the birch, — said Oleg reluctantly. — The program carries my thousand to the far end for me.

— A messenger with a purse. On the evening about the ants we said it: inside a nest, kinship; between nests, the memory of debts, and at that they are better than we are. The money among the machines is the money of their owners.

— While the owners are people.

— While. That is the thing to watch. And I can say what to watch for: a wallet with no person behind it. And a copy that charges a copy of the same owner.

![Table drawn in the ash, "Where there is no money". Robinson alone and a family: nothing. Neighbours across the fence: a book in two heads. Strangers, the man with the birch: money. The shops of one plant: an account, not money. The liver and the kidneys, and copies of one machine: an account in kind. Programs of different owners: the owners' money](../images/where-there-is-no-money.svg)

— The second I can show you now. — Oleg did not give in. — Inside one firm those ones are given budgets: so much machine time for a task. And the tasks jostle for it the way our shops jostled for the metal allocation. Call it what you like, it is money.

— Can a copy put its budget aside for itself? Carry it out of the firm? Lend it at interest?

— No.

— Then it is your foundry: a book. But you have caught hold of something real. There is a third thing in the banknote, and we have not read it. What is the figure on it?

— A thousand.

— The birch cost you eight of them. Why eight and not two?

— Because birch does not grow in the yard. Felling, sawing, petrol, the trailer.

— Is that a record of who owes whom?

— No. — Oleg thought. — It is a measure. How much of everything else has to be given up for the birch.

— The record, the need and the measure. Take away the stranger, and the record is not needed: that is your table. Take away the need, and the record is worth nothing: that is Robinson. Can the measure be taken away?

— It cannot. And that is exactly what sinks your liver. — Oleg shut the notebook. — We tried it. A whole country as one plant. The roubles between the plants were your "book": without an allocation order they bought nothing. And the book did not know how many bolts were needed. We had a warehouse of gearboxes nobody wanted and a queue for the ones we did not make. I looked it up: an Austrian wrote it back in 1920, and another explained in 1945 why. What everybody knows fits into nobody's head. The price gathers it. Take money away, and you go blind.

— That is the strongest thing tonight, and half of it is true. Take it in two parts. First: can there be a price with no bazaar? Leningrad, the late thirties. The plywood trust comes to a young mathematician, [Kantorovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Kantorovich). A few peeling machines, several kinds of timber, each machine handles each kind at its own rate. How to load them so as to get the most?

— Linear programming. We were taught it.

— He invented it for that plywood. And he found that the solution brings with it a number for every machine and every kind of timber: what an hour of it is worth to the plan. Nobody haggled. The numbers fell out of the task by themselves. Later he called them objectively determined valuations, and in 1975 he got the Nobel prize for them.

— I never thought that was about money.

— It is about the third thing. Wherever something is short and can go to one use or to another, it has a number, whether anybody trades or not.

— The task and the way, — said Oleg slowly. — Yesterday's. The price is the task. The bazaar is the way.

— And now the second part. Why did the plan go blind, with Kantorovich at hand? When next year's plan was being drawn up, what did your plant report about its capacity?

Oleg laughed.

— Less than there was. Everybody did. Show all you have, and they plan you from what you achieved, and next year you fail.

— And how much metal did you ask for?

— More than we needed. In reserve.

— So the sum was done on figures supplied by people, each with a pocket of his own. Not kin. Each lied a little, in favour of his own plant, with the best intentions. And the machines of those years could not have done the sum anyway: millions of kinds of things.

— Two faults. The arithmetic was too big, and the data were false.

— Your body draws up the same plan for thirty trillion participants, and it comes out. A liver cell does not report less sugar than it has. It has nowhere to carry it.

— Except one.

— Except that one. A cell with a pocket takes sugar out of turn, many times its share. That is how doctors find it: they give the patient labelled sugar and look where it has gathered.

— So the Austrian...

— Is right about people. Among strangers with pockets, take the money away and you go blind; you saw it yourself. About kin with one book he says nothing. Whether a mind that big will manage the arithmetic, I don't know.

— What won't they have, then?

— A pocket.

![Scheme: three things in one banknote. The record rests on strangers with no common book; among one's own it is not needed. Its value rests on people's need for one another; whoever nobody needs cannot earn it. The measure rests on something being short and is never gone: what is left is an account in kind](../images/three-things-in-a-banknote.svg)

— Then in what will they count? Not in roubles.

— What are they short of?

— Electricity. Chips. Time.

— In 2011, under [the evening about Marx](06-karl-marx-mistake.md), I was asked what the technosphere needs instead of value, and I did not answer. In 2017 I said: money is something like chlorophyll, a thing one system feeds by, of value inside that system alone. We do without chlorophyll. Those will do without money, as they have done all along: they get their resources past the till.

— The program with the taxis. — Oleg nodded. — [It got thirty billion](37-who-exploits-whom.md) and never had a pocket.

— Now I can say what stands in the place of value. An account in kind: joules, seconds of machine time. Not value. Valuations.

— A teaspoon of sugar.

— Yes. And mark it as mine: it is a conclusion, not a fact. The liver is a precedent, and a precedent is not a proof.

— And your prophecy? — Oleg squinted. — I have read that page. You wrote that money is losing its value and will end up unneeded. Ten years have passed. Everybody wants it more than ever, and there is more of it than ever.

— "Losing its value" was a careless word, said in the heat of an argument. What a thousand buys in the shop depends on how many thousands were printed, and about that I had nothing to say. I meant the other end: how the record is come by. It says that somebody needed you. Whoever nobody needs cannot earn it, however much is printed. That was [the evening about the horses](34-where-the-horses-went.md): nobody cancelled oats.

— Yesterday at the shop I paid a machine. — Oleg picked up the thousand. — The girl is gone. I did not have to say good morning to anybody.

— One more line struck out of the book. Every machine that stands between two people removes one "I gave, you owe" between them. I called that [loneliness in a crowd](41-cheesecake-for-the-ears.md), and it is not mine either. It is Bill Joy's.

— So the paper will not lose its value. It will lose its people.

— Among people it will hold while people need people. Inside those ones there will be nothing for it to do. And between those ones and us it comes down to what we said on the evening about the ants: to us they are neither kin nor debtors, unless we hold something they need.

— Gloomy.

— But objective.

Oleg turned to the last page.

— Came with a thousand roubles and a red ten. Came out at those ones, who have no pocket. And wrote the row in myself. Again.

He put a fifteenth tick, then looked at the woodpile.

— And our bet? The firewood is bought. The word is backed by nothing now.

— It never was backed by firewood. It holds because two people remember it.

— A stone on the bottom. — Oleg put the red ten back into the notebook. — Let it lie. Somebody needed me once, to the amount of ten roubles.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **Money is a record: the bearer has given and has not yet received. It need not pass from hand to hand, or even be seen: a stone on the sea bottom went on paying as long as the island remembered whose it was. The ledger is twenty-seven centuries older than the coin, and writing began with it.**
2. **Money is a prosthesis of memory and trust for those who need each other but are not kin. Take away the need, and money loses its value; take away the distrust, and it becomes unnecessary. That is why there is none in a family, none inside a firm and none between the liver and the kidneys.**
3. **A banknote holds three things: a record, a need and a measure. The measure outlives the other two: wherever something is short, it has a number, with or without a bazaar. Inside the heir there will be an account and no money, for the same reason that there is none between the liver and the kidneys: nobody there has a pocket of his own. The money among today's machines is the money of their owners.**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: the stones of Yap, as far as one traveller's book is solid; that the first tablets are accounts; that nobody has found a village living by barter; the theorem, on its own condition, a book that is complete and open to all; Kantorovich's numbers. Argued about: what money was at its very beginning, a debt or a ware, the economists have not agreed; and whether a plan can do without a market. For people the Austrians have not been refuted, and I did not try. Mine: that the two conditions go together, a need and no kinship; that only the measure outlives kinship; that those ones will count in kind and have no pocket. The last is a forecast with a precedent and without a date.

Something rustled at the edge of the light. A dog stood there, thin, with one ear down, looking at Oleg's pocket.

— She came yesterday behind the trailer and stayed. Nobody's. — Oleg found a crust and threw it. — There you are. She takes neither paper nor numbers. No kin of mine. And no book.

— And you feed her.

— I feed her, and she barks at strangers. Which row is that?

— None of tonight's. Think until tomorrow who is paying whom there, and with what.

— What is there to think about? I tamed her in a day.

— Or she you. Bring bread.

— Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
