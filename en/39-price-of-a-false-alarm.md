# Oleg's Prophetic Dream and the Price of a False Alarm

Oleg came earlier than usual and did not sit down. Andrey was slicing potatoes into a frying pan.

— Explain that, determinist.

— Explain what?

— Yesterday you told me to sleep attentively. I did. I dreamt of Genka. We shared a desk from the fifth form to the eighth. I haven't seen him for thirty years and haven't thought of him for ten. We stood in the school corridor, and he was asking me for a ruler. At nine this morning the phone rings, an unknown number. "Oleg? It's Genka."

— What did he want?

— Later. First tell me where this goes in that world of yours, where everything is counted in advance.

Andrey put the pan on the coals.

— And if he had not called, would you have told me this dream?

— What is there to tell? A corridor, a ruler.

— So tonight I would have heard nothing.

— Nothing.

— And how many times in thirty years have I heard nothing?

— That's unfair, — said Oleg, and sat down. — Nobody tells the dreams that didn't come true.

— Then let's count them without telling. Take out the notebook. Thirty years: how many nights?

— Thirty by three hundred and sixty-five. About eleven thousand.

— How many people like Genka do you have? Known once and lost since.

— The school. The institute. The army. Three jobs, the old yard. — Oleg bent his fingers, then gave up. — Three hundred, no fewer.

— How often does one of the three hundred turn up? Phones, writes, runs into you at the market, or somebody tells you news of him.

— Now, with all these networks... Five times a year.

— One day in seventy. And how often do you wake up remembering that one of the three hundred was in your dream?

— I remember dreams badly. Once a month.

— Twelve a year, three hundred and sixty in thirty years. Now take one such morning. You wake with one of them in your head. What is the chance that somebody of the three hundred turns up that day?

— One in seventy.

— And that it is the same man?

— One in three hundred. — Oleg multiplied. — One in twenty-one thousand.

— For one dream. You had three hundred and sixty.

— Three hundred and sixty out of twenty-one thousand... — Oleg looked up, pleased. — One chance in fifty-eight. Say sixty. You wanted to show me there was nothing in it, and you got one in sixty. I wouldn't put money on such a horse.

— Neither would I. Now three questions. If he had phoned not this morning but tomorrow evening, would you have come to me with it?

— I would. "I dreamt of him, and the next day..."

— And in a week?

— "That very week." — Oleg grunted. — Yes.

— Then the window is a week, not a morning. Multiply by seven.

— One in eight and a bit.

— Second. If it had been your wife's dream and her classmate?

— She would have told me before I opened my eyes. And I'd have brought it here.

— How many people are there whose story of this kind you would have brought here?

— The family, friends, the neighbours at the dacha. Forty.

— Forty people, each with one chance in eight.

— Five stories. — Oleg put the pencil down. — In thirty years I should have heard five of them.

— Have you?

— My grandmother. My wife's aunt, who saw her brother the night before the telegram. A neighbour, with his bathhouse that burnt down. — He counted. — About that many.

— And the third. This morning's version, the strict one: a call the same morning, to you personally. One in sixty in thirty years is what in one year?

— One in eighteen hundred.

— There are more than a hundred million adults in the country.

— Fifty-five thousand a year. — Oleg wrote. — A hundred and fifty a day.

— This morning about a hundred and fifty people in the country got a call from somebody they had dreamt of in the night. You are one of them. The other hundred and forty-nine are telling it now at other fires.

— And the hundred million who got no call are telling nothing.

— The numbers are yours, and they are taken from the ceiling. Halve any of them or double it; the order stays. A Cambridge mathematician, Littlewood, counted more roughly still, and [his law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littlewood%27s_law) is retold like this: a man awake notices something about once a second, a million events a month. So a thing with one chance in a million should happen to each of us about once a month.

— A miracle a month. On schedule.

![Scheme: three hundred and sixty dots in thirty columns of twelve, one for every dream of an old acquaintance that Oleg remembered in the morning, once a month for thirty years. All the dots are grey except one: the dream after which the phone rang. The grey ones are written off and not one of them can be named; the one is remembered for life](../images/dreams-and-one-call.svg)

— Now name one of the other three hundred and fifty-nine dreams.

— I can't.

— Where are they?

— Written off. — Oleg remembered. — The stocktaking. [The first evening](33-use-of-sleep.md): at night the light images go and the heavy ones stay.

— And this one was given a weight this morning that will last you for life. So your ledger has an income column and no expense column. Not because you are a dishonest bookkeeper. The warehouse is built that way.

— Still, — said Oleg. — One in sixty. I feel it wasn't for nothing, and the feeling doesn't go away from your arithmetic.

— How many of you were there in the class?

— Thirty.

— What is the chance that two of the thirty had the same birthday?

— Thirty out of three hundred and sixty-five. Under a tenth.

— Seventy percent.

— It can't be.

— Did you have such a pair?

Oleg was silent for a while.

— Lyuda and Vovka. The twelfth of April, Cosmonautics Day. The whole school thought it a marvel.

— You counted the chance that somebody shares a birthday with you. Nobody asked about you. Any two. How many pairs can be made of thirty people?

— Thirty by twenty-nine, halved. Four hundred and thirty-five.

— Four hundred and thirty-five tickets, each with one chance in three hundred and sixty-five. With twenty-three people a shared birthday is [already more likely than not](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birthday_problem); with thirty it is seven in ten.

— So I counted from myself.

— Everybody does. "What is the chance that this happens to me, with him, tonight" is a small number. "That something like this happens to somebody at some time" is close to one. The first number is about you, the second is about the world. The marvel lives in the gap between them.

— Anthropocentrism in a pocket edition.

— Your word.

![Chart: the chance that at least two people in a group share a birthday, against the size of the group. With 23 people it is 50.7 percent (253 pairs), with 30 people 70.6 percent (435 pairs), with 57 people 99 percent. A second, low curve shows the chance that somebody in the group shares your own birthday: 7.6 percent in a group of 30](../images/shared-birthday.svg)

— Now, what did Genka want?

— He is gathering the class. In June it is forty-five years since we left school.

— Did you know it was forty-five?

— I can count. And my wife said back in the winter: your lot have a round date this year.

— So the date has been sitting in two heads since the winter. In yours it pulled out the corridor and the ruler. In his, the telephone.

— A common cause.

— Two cocks crow at dawn together, and neither has woken the other. In the weeks before a round date both the dreams and the calls come several times more often than our arithmetic allowed.

— But why this very night?

— Who made last night the night to sleep attentively?

— You did. — Oleg stopped. — I lay down waiting for a dream. Otherwise I wouldn't have remembered it at all.

— That is one more link. The rest I can't trace.

— So it's chance after all, determinist?

— The question is badly posed. For me nothing happens by chance: your dream had its causes, his call had its own, and both chains go back to one beginning, [we talked about that](17-global-determinism.md). "Chance" is the name for causes I can't trace. The real question is different: is there a short road from the dream to the call, or only the long one, through the common past?

— And?

— One short road we have found: the date. A road straight from the dream to the call I don't see. That I don't see it is a fact about me, not about the road. And whether tomorrow can be remembered at all is a separate evening.

— And my grandmother? Hers came true all the time, without any dates.

— What did she dream?

— Raw meat: someone will fall ill. A tooth falling out with blood: a death among the kin. Fish: somebody is expecting.

— How large was the family?

— With the cousins and the in-laws, about forty.

— In a family of forty, how long do you wait for an illness?

— Till Thursday. — Oleg laughed. — And she set no term. If nobody fell ill within the week, the dream went on waiting.

— And if nobody did at all?

— Then she had prayed it away.

— A forecast with no date, and no outcome that could refute it. It can't help coming true.

— And you gave me dates and odds last autumn.

— For that very reason.

— All right. But why are we built so stupidly? Half the village knocks on wood. My niece, a grown woman with a degree, when she was a student stuck her record book out of the window at midnight before every exam and shouted "Freebie, come!"

— And did it come?

— She passed.

— And those who shouted and failed?

— They shouted badly.

— Is there a smoke detector in your flat?

— In the kitchen. It screams at every cutlet. My wife fans it with a towel.

— How many times has it screamed in all these years?

— A hundred. More.

— And how many fires?

— None, touch wood... — Oleg looked at his knuckles, already raised over the log, and took his hand away. — None.

— Why not turn the screw so that it screams less often?

— Then it will sleep through the real one.

— Could one be made that never screams at a cutlet and never sleeps through a fire?

— Out of smoke alone? No. Smoke is smoke. Either it is sensitive or it is quiet.

— So there are two mistakes, a false alarm and a miss, and one screw between them: fewer of the one means more of the other. Where do you set it?

— By the price. A cutlet costs a towel. A fire costs the flat.

Andrey drew four squares in the ash with a twig.

— Now the grass. Something rustles. It is a snake or it is the wind; you jump away or you stay. Fill them in.

— Wind, and I stayed: nothing. Wind, and I jumped: a minute lost, and I look a fool. Snake, and I jumped: alive. Snake, and I stayed. — Oleg pointed the pencil at the fourth square. — No more entries in the ledger.

— Put prices on them. A jump costs a minute. Say a life is worth a hundred thousand such minutes; it is worth more.

— Then it pays to jump even if only one rustle in a thousand is a snake. In ten thousand.

— Nine hundred and ninety-nine jumps for nothing, and you are in profit. An American doctor, [Randolph Nesse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randolph_M._Nesse), called it the smoke detector principle: a defence that goes off for nothing most of the time may be tuned exactly right. In 2009 two biologists [wrote it as a formula](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2008.0981): selection keeps the habit of linking things that are not linked, as long as a link missed costs more than a link invented.

— So we descend from those who jumped.

— From those who jumped at the wind. Those who waited for proof were right more often, and left fewer children.

— So the scream comes before the proof.

— It has to. We met it on [the evening about wants](18-anatomy-of-wants.md): when the scales are level, an emotion decides for you and then defends the decision.

— The watchdog.

— Here it barks before the scales have even been brought.

![Scheme: the four squares Andrey drew in the ash. Something rustles in the grass. If it was the wind and you jumped away, it is a false alarm and costs a minute; if it was the wind and you stayed, nothing is lost; if it was a snake and you jumped away, you are alive; if it was a snake and you stayed, it is a miss and costs a life. If a life is worth 100,000 such minutes, it pays to jump even when only one rustle in 10,000 is a snake](../images/price-of-two-mistakes.svg)

— That is fear. And the omens?

— The same screw, only what is at stake is a lesson and not a snake. Is there a dish you can't eat?

— Sprats in tomato sauce. In 1985 I was sick all night on a train after a tin of them. It was the flu, as it turned out; half the carriage went down with it. I know that. I still can't.

— One coincidence, and for forty years. Rats are the same. Let a rat feel sick once, hours after a new taste, and it [will not touch that taste again](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditioned_taste_aversion), even if the sickness came from X-rays and not from the food.

— A superstition of the stomach.

— It costs you the sprats. It saved your ancestors from every poisonous root they survived once. To learn is to link what came together before you know why. Whoever links at the first coincidence learns fast and sometimes knocks on wood. Whoever waits for a hundred coincidences knocks on nothing and is poisoned at the second.

— And is there anybody who links with no snake at all? For nothing?

— In 1948 an American psychologist, [Skinner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._F._Skinner), put eight hungry pigeons in boxes. A feeder opened every fifteen seconds, whatever the bird did. In a few days six of the eight had a ritual. One turned anticlockwise, two or three turns between feedings. Another thrust its head into an upper corner of the box. A third tossed its head as if lifting a bar that wasn't there. Two swung like pendulums.

— They were calling the food.

— The food came while the bird happened to be turning. So it turned more, and so the food found it turning again. Skinner called the paper "'Superstition' in the pigeon", with the word in quotation marks.

— "Freebie, come."

— And the bird that went hungry had turned badly. To be fair: in 1971 two researchers repeated the experiment, watched more closely and argued with his reading. Nearer to the feeding all the birds came to pecking, and the odd dances filled the wait in between. What exactly the pigeon links is still argued about. That a feeder indifferent to the bird produces stable rituals nobody disputes.

— A pigeon, a rat and my grandmother, — said Oleg. — Fine company you have put me in. Now listen. You have said it yourself more than once: practice stands above theory. This is practice. It happened, to me, this morning. And your three hundred and your one in seventy are an excuse, and one that can't be checked. With arithmetic like that you can count away anything at all.

— I won't argue. The arithmetic doesn't prove that it was a coincidence. It proves a weaker thing: that a coincidence is enough. So the dream by itself proves nothing in either direction.

— Evasive.

— Then here is a case I can't count away. [My own](../sources/garya/proza/podpolshchiki.md).

— Yours?

— I was about eighteen. A friend of mine was an apprentice electrician, and out of boredom we played cards in the tool store. We noticed that, looking into each other's eyes, we often guessed the other's cards. And we kept starting the same phrase in chorus. I had read the books of [Vasiliev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonid_Vasiliev), a Leningrad physiologist who studied telepathy, and we set up a test with a deck. Eye to eye we guessed up to nine times in ten. When one of us went into the next room, six in ten.

— Six in ten through a wall. — Oleg whistled. — And you kept quiet about it for seven evenings?

— With two other friends the same test gave nothing. Then one day we tried it properly, with the [five cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zener_cards) made for it: a circle, a cross, a star, a square, waves. A day off, at my place, the family out. We sat at opposite ends of the room, so that the picture couldn't be read from the reflection in an eye. The window looked west, behind a net curtain, so the light was diffuse. I took the circle and began to send. And a long shadow slid down from the window along the wall under the sill, crossed the floor without a sound and went into the gap under the door to the hall.

— You both saw it?

— "Did you see that? — I did. What was it?" We looked out of the window: nothing. I opened the door: nothing in the hall. I said a bird had flown past. He said: fetch something and make me that shadow.

— And?

Andrey took the frying pan off the coals and held it up between the fire and the spruce.

— I fetched a frying pan from the kitchen. First: to get a shadow on the wall under the sill we had to hold the pan almost against the wall, inside the room. A bird outside can't put one there. Second: the pan's shadow was pale and blurred, and the parquet showed through it. That thing was black, with sharp edges. Third: move the pan so that its shadow reaches the door.

— It climbs the trunk, — said Oleg, looking at the spruce.

— A shadow climbs up the door. That one went under it. It was about four metres long and shaped like a skittle moving knob first.

— And what was it?

— I don't know.

— Forty-five years, and you don't know?

— I know what it was not. Not the shadow of anything outside the window: that we checked, and I leave it one chance in twenty, for the mistakes of two boys with a frying pan. Past that line my list of candidates is empty, and without a list there is nothing to share the chances among.

— And the cards? Nine in ten?

— There I have a list. In Berlin in 1904 a retired teacher showed a horse, [Hans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clever_Hans), that did sums: it tapped out the answer with a hoof. A commission of thirteen, with a circus manager and the director of the zoo on it, found no trick. Then a young psychologist, Pfungst, tried one thing: let the man who asks not know the answer.

— And the horse?

— While the questioner knew the answer, Hans was right about nine times in ten. When he didn't, hardly ever. The horse was watching the man. As the taps came up to the right number the man relaxed, by a hair, and Hans stopped. The owner had no idea that he was prompting. Pfungst, who knew, could not stop doing it himself.

— Nine in ten. Like you and your electrician.

— I noticed. I won't build a theory on that coincidence. But eye to eye I'd put seven chances in ten on the horse.

— And through the wall? A horse doesn't see through a wall.

— Through the wall there is the chorus. We said the same phrases with no cards at all: two heads filled in the same yard pick alike. If the sender picked the card himself and didn't draw it by lot, I'd guess him more often than chance allows with no message between us. The two cocks again. Did we draw by lot in the tool store? I wouldn't swear to it now. And the count was kept by the two who wanted it to work.

— So everything is explained.

— No. Everything is a list. Seven in ten on the horse and the two cocks together. Two on a generous count. One I leave to what I can't name. And the shadow isn't on the list at all.

— That is not an answer.

— It is the only honest one I have. "Not explained" doesn't mean "can't be explained". And the reverse holds as well: "can be explained by chance" doesn't mean "was chance". I've carried that shadow for forty-five years and haven't hung a label on it, neither "miracle" nor "nonsense".

— And practice above theory?

— Practice is what can be repeated, or at least was written down before the outcome. One case told afterwards is a recollection. Mine too.

— Then how do I find out? About mine.

— You are holding the instrument. Put the notebook by the bed. In the morning, before you touch the telephone, write down who was in the dream. All of them, the corridor and the ruler too. In a year count: how many entries, how many calls.

— And if it's ten calls where you promise none?

— Then I go for firewood without having said the word, and we start again from the first evening.

— I'll hold you to that. — Oleg wrote on a clean page: "Dreams. Before the phone." — So is this the whole of science? A notebook where the dreams that didn't come true are kept?

— The greater part of it. Written before, checked after, counted all. Memory can't do it: it writes the expenses off the same night.

Oleg turned the pencil in his fingers.

— Do you know who took it more calmly than anybody? Before coming here I asked the chatbot: I dreamt of a classmate and he rang, what does it mean? It gave me your arithmetic almost word for word, birthdays included. Not a shiver. There is a mind without a grandmother: no snake, no fear, no omens.

— It was you who brought it to the fire.

— I did. I'm only saying it is lucky.

— In 2019, in Berlin, the city of Hans as it happens, researchers [looked at](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-019-08987-4) what a program that recognised photographs was actually looking at when it said "a horse". It had done well in a well-known exam. About a fifth of the horse photographs in its exercise book came from one archive, which stamped its name in the corner.

— Don't tell me.

— It had learned the stamp. They cut the stamp out, and the horse stopped being a horse. They pasted it on a car, and the car became a horse.

— A horse again. — Oleg shook his head. — The stamp in the corner was its black cat.

— They called such programs "Clever Hans" themselves. And another case. In 2008 Google began to [forecast the flu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_Flu_Trends) from what people typed into the search. Fifty million phrases were tried against the doctors' record of past years, a little over a thousand points, and the best fitting were kept. Among the best were phrases about school basketball. The engineers threw those out by hand.

— What has basketball to do with it?

— The season is the same: winter. They threw out the ones they recognised. By the winter of 2012–13 the thing showed twice as many sick as the doctors counted.

— Fifty million phrases against a thousand points. — Oleg looked at his notebook. — That is my three hundred classmates and eleven thousand nights, only without a conscience. Search that widely, and a classmate will be found for any dream.

— The same arithmetic. Whoever looks for links among many things on few examples will find links that are not there. It doesn't matter what he is made of.

— But it has no snake. What is it afraid of?

— I don't know that it is afraid of anything, and I don't claim it. It has prices. While it learns, it is rewarded for every link that fits the exercise book. A false link that fits is paid exactly like a true one, and the bill comes later, on other photographs. And the talking ones: in 2025 the makers of one of them [wrote out](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.04664) why it invents an answer instead of saying "I don't know". In the exams "I don't know" gets nought, and a guess sometimes gets a point.

— An answer missed costs a point, an answer invented costs nothing. — Oleg slapped the notebook. — The snake and the minute. So that is where my book with the author, the year and the publisher came from.

— Only this time the prices were set by us, not by the grass.

— So it will have omens of its own.

— It has them already: the stamp and the basketball. The task is the same for anybody who learns: on incomplete data, to choose which mistake to make more often. Fear, the dream book and knocking on wood are our way of carrying that tilt. Its way will be different. What it will be I don't know.

— And which of us is the more superstitious?

— It can do what you can't: keep all three hundred and sixty dreams and count them. Where somebody examines it in a new place, it will be less superstitious than we are. Where nobody does, more, and at fifty million phrases a time.

— And who will examine it when we no longer can?

— That is a separate evening.

Oleg leafed back through the notebook and stopped.

— Now about you. Seven evenings. I ask about sleep, horses, soup, laughter, losses, air, and today about a dream. And every time you see one and the same thing behind it. One feeder, seven dances. How are you better than the pigeon?

— That is the best question of this spring. I may be no better.

— And that's all?

— For tonight. The pigeon doesn't write down the times the food didn't come. I gave you dates and odds, and they lie in your notebook, written before the outcome. Whether that is enough we shall see. Put the question on the last page and don't let me off it.

— I won't. — Oleg wrote it down, then read aloud what he had added below. — Came with a prophetic dream. Came out at a machine with a black cat in the corner of a photograph. Again.

He put a seventh tick beside the six.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **A prophetic dream is arithmetic counted from oneself. For one man it is a rare thing, one chance in sixty in thirty years; in the country it happens to a hundred and fifty people every morning. The dreams that did not come true are written off the same night, so memory keeps an income column and no expense column.**
2. **The correlator is tuned for false alarms: a regularity missed cost a life, one invented cost a minute. Superstition is the price of the ability to learn, and it is paid by whatever learns on unequal prices: the pigeon, the horse's master, the machine.**
3. **The honest answer to the unexplained: write it down before the phone rings, estimate the probability and leave the line open. "Not explained" does not mean "inexplicable", and "chance is enough" does not mean "it was chance".**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: the pairs of birthdays, the screw of the detector, the stamp on the horse photographs, the flu counted twice over. Argued about: what the pigeon really links. Yours: the three hundred, the one in seventy and the hundred and fifty every morning; they give the order and no more. Mine: that our omens and the machine's stamps are one and the same tilt. I believe so; I haven't proved it. And open: the shadow. And your Genka, until the notebook is full.

— Gloomy, — said Oleg. — I had one miracle, and you divided it by sixty.

— I left it to you. One in sixty is a rare thing. I only said that it isn't a message.

— Is that objective?

— To within the ceiling the numbers were taken from.

Andrey put the pan back on the coals and stirred the potatoes.

— And what did Genka tell you, besides the date?

— The news. Our plant has been shut for twenty years; half the class worked there. I thought the town had died with it. He says it stands, and has even grown. The plant is closed and the town stands. How is that? I've put it in the notebook.

— Then count something for tomorrow. How many times does a mouse's heart beat in its life, and how many times an elephant's?

— What has a mouse to do with a town?

— Tomorrow.

— And the dream? Do I write it down tonight?

— In the morning. Before the telephone.

— Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
