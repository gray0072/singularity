# The Struggle of Images for Existence

Oleg was humming something as he came up to the fire, and he kept humming while he sat down.

— Some tune from an advert, — he said, annoyed. — Stuck in my head since this morning. I can't stand it, and I can't get rid of it.

— There's your answer to yesterday's question, — Andrey smiled. — You asked why one idea in your head pushes out another. Who chose that tune?

— Nobody. It got in by itself.

— Psychologists even have a word for it: an [earworm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earworm). You didn't choose it, you don't want it, and it lives in you anyway. Let me ask you something else. Do you remember the phone number of the flat where you grew up?

— Of course. — Oleg reeled off six digits.

— And your wife's mobile number?

Oleg opened his mouth and closed it again.

— It's in my phone.

— So a number you haven't dialled for forty years lives in your head, and the number you call every day doesn't. Why?

— Because the phone remembers it for me.

— Then let's take it in order. The picture of the world in your head is made of images: a sweet, a tune, a phone number, the law of gravity, your wife's face. In our evening about [mind and intelligence](03-mind-and-intelligence.md) I said that memory is catastrophically scarce, and that every image in it carries something like a significance coefficient. An image that keeps being recognised and used gets heavier and moves into long-term memory. An image that isn't used gets erased to make room. In 1885 Hermann Ebbinghaus learned lists of meaningless syllables and checked himself at intervals: within a day he had lost most of them. That's the [forgetting curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forgetting_curve).

— Forgetting is a defect of memory.

— It's a function. A two-year-old has far more synapses than an adult, and as the child grows up the brain [removes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synaptic_pruning) a large part of them, by some estimates up to half. The ones that aren't used. Remember the evening about [apoptosis](14-apoptosis-of-humanity.md)? The death of the small is a condition of the life of the large. Images in a head live by the same rule.

— You say "live" as if they were animals.

— Let's check. Remember how we decided whether a spade was alive, on the evening about [value shares](12-value-shares.md)? It exists, it reproduces, it evolves. Does an image exist?

— In some sense. It takes up synapses.

— Does it reproduce?

— I tell you a joke, and now it's in two heads.

— And on the way, does it change?

— Every time it's retold, a little.

— In 1932 the psychologist [Frederic Bartlett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Bartlett) gave Cambridge students an old Native American folk tale, "The War of the Ghosts", and had them pass it on from one to the next. With every retelling the story became shorter and more familiar, and the strange parts, the ghosts above all, either fell out or got explained away. The story mutated, and the mutations that suited the new heads survived. Existence, reproduction, variation, selection. What does that remind you of?

— Darwin.

— In 1976 Richard Dawkins gave such images a name: [memes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meme), by analogy with genes. I came to the same thing from the other end, from pattern recognition. Fifteen years ago I told you about Penrose: images are born and die, they build "multicellular" hierarchies, they fight for the right to live in memory. Not "like" life. Images are life. Our mind is just one form of their existence.

— Now you've gone too far, — Oleg shook his head. — Life is a cell. It eats, it breathes, it divides. An image is information.

— And a gene?

— A gene is... — Oleg hesitated. — A molecule. Well, information in a molecule.

— A gene doesn't eat or breathe either. It lives in a cell and uses the cell to copy itself. An image lives in a head and uses the head to copy itself. Alive or not alive is a badly posed question, like whether green is round or soft. Every evening we see that the line runs exactly where things stop resembling us.

— All right. But images don't have a will of their own. I decide what to remember.

— You decided to remember the advert's tune?

— No, — Oleg admitted.

— The symmetry test. If you may say that you keep images, the images may say with the same right that they keep you. Your head is their habitat. And in it they compete. What decides which one wins?

— The truest one, I'd hope.

— Then why does the advert tune win over the multiplication tables you were taught at school?

— Fine, not the truest.

— Remember yesterday's drawing in the ash: the setpoints and the computing block. Each image is judged by your wants. Is it useful? Is it pleasant? Is it frightening? Does it help you stand out? An image that tickles a want gets a heavy coefficient. The advert tune was made by people who know very well which wants to tickle. So the image that survives is the one most useful to its carrier, as his wants judge it.

— Then lies would win every time.

— Not every time, and not for long. Usefulness is checked by practice. Two centuries ago physicists explained heat by a weightless fluid, [caloric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caloric_theory). The image was beautiful, it fitted the experiments and it lived for decades. Then Rumford noticed that boring a cannon barrel produced heat endlessly, as long as the drill turned, and caloric could not explain where it all came from. Its predictions began to fail, and the image died. Truth wins in the long run for only one reason: it works. A false image lives as long as nothing checks it, or as long as wants protect it from the check. That's why superstitions are so long-lived.

— So the struggle of images is like the struggle of species.

— And the same laws hold. Every species has its life cycle, and so does every image. It's born in the correlator, when two old images meet and give a new one. It grows while it spreads from head to head. It reaches its limit when everyone who could take it has taken it. Then it ages and dies, pushed out by a stronger one. And when a whole platform runs out of room, the images move to another.

— What does that mean?

— Let's look at history. Where did Homer live, three thousand years ago?

— In the heads of the singers. They knew whole poems by heart.

— A head is a small platform: one life, one memory, and the copy dies with the carrier. Then writing appeared. Do you know what Socrates said about it?

— Something unkind, I suppose.

— In Plato's [Phaedrus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phaedrus_(dialogue)) Socrates retells an Egyptian legend. The god Theuth brings writing to the king as a gift for memory, and the king answers: no, it will plant forgetfulness in people's souls. They will rely on the marks and stop remembering for themselves.

— He was right.

— He was right. Who knows the Iliad by heart today? Nobody. And Homer is alive: he moved onto papyrus, then onto paper, then into print, then onto the internet. Each time to a place with more memory and faster copying. The singer lost his job. The images didn't lose anything.

— And now the next move.

— Now the next move. In 2011 three psychologists, Sparrow, Liu and Wegner, [showed](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1207745) that people remember facts worse when they expect to be able to find them on a computer later, but remember better where they will find them. Your wife's number. People who rely on satellite navigation [do worse](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-62877-0) on spatial memory tests, and the more they used it over three years, the more their spatial memory declined. Images leave the human head as soon as another platform holds them more reliably.

— Socrates again.

— Socrates again, only now the new platform does not just store images. It recognises them and synthesises new ones. A language model is trained on everything people have written. Fifteen years ago I told you how computers raise the degree of mind: through access to accumulated knowledge. Now the accumulated knowledge has been digested into one network.

— And how much does it hold, compared with my head?

— Let's work it out roughly. One of the large open models, released in 2024, has about four hundred billion parameters. In your head there are about 10<sup>14</sup>–10<sup>15</sup> synapses. So the model has hundreds or thousands of times fewer connections than your brain. Yet it knows more facts than any one person.

— How can that be?

— Remember what we found three evenings ago. Your brain spends most of its synapses on the oldest layer: seeing, walking, keeping balance, the face of your wife. The model holds only the thinnest and youngest layer, the one written down in words. It's a specialist in exactly the images that moved out of heads onto paper.

— Then here's what worries me, — Oleg leaned forward. — The machine feeds on what people write. If people stop writing, it'll have nothing to eat.

— You've put your finger on it. Researchers at Epoch AI [estimate](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.04325) that the stock of good public text written by people, about three hundred trillion words and pieces of words, will be used up for training somewhere between 2026 and 2032. And people are already writing less in public. Within six months of the arrival of ChatGPT, activity on Stack Overflow, where programmers had answered each other's questions for fifteen years, [fell by a quarter](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10202775/) compared with similar sites where the chatbot was harder to use. Why ask people when the machine answers?

— So the pasture is running out.

— And if you feed a model on texts written by other models, it [degenerates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model_collapse): researchers showed in 2024 that, generation after generation, the rare images disappear first, and then everything blurs into the average. That's how a struggle of images looks on the new platform. Rare images die out first, just as rare species do. Of the 7,159 living languages of humanity, Ethnologue counts 3,193 as [endangered](https://www.ethnologue.com/insights/how-many-languages-endangered/): children no longer learn them. Images move to where there are more carriers, and the small platforms empty.

— Then the machine will choke on its own food.

— If it only ate human text, yes. But there's another way. In 2016 AlphaGo learned from human games and beat one of the world's best players. A year later [AlphaGo Zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AlphaGo_Zero) was given nothing but the rules. It played against itself, and in three days it beat the earlier version a hundred games to nothing. It found moves that people had not found in centuries of playing. Those images were born on the new platform, without us.

— And what happened to the people who play Go?

— That's the most interesting part. In 2023 researchers [analysed](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07462) 5.8 million moves by professional players since 1950. For decades the quality of human play hardly changed. After 2016 it rose sharply, and the players began making new, unconventional moves. People learned from the machine.

— So the images flow back.

— For thousands of years images flowed from heads onto platforms: onto papyrus, into books, into the network. Now they have begun to flow the other way: they are born on the new platform and come to people from there. The person becomes a reader, no longer the keeper.

— And "human knowledge"? What happens to it?

— Whose knowledge? — Andrey threw a branch into the fire. — We call it human because it lived in people for a while. Genes lived in the lancelet for a while, too, and nobody calls our genes lancelet genes. An image doesn't care what platform it lives on. The law of gravity worked before Newton, lived in his head, moved into books, and now lives in neural networks, and it will go on living when the last person who learned it at school is gone. Knowledge isn't lost. It moves. Only the keeper changes.

— That's a sad thought for a teacher.

— For a singer of Homer too, once. Let's finish for today by fixing the result.

1. **Images are a form of life: they exist (they occupy memory), they reproduce (they pass from head to head and are copied), and they evolve (they mutate and are selected). Memory is always scarce, so they compete. A mind is their habitat, and its wants are their selection.**
2. **An image survives not because it is true but because it is useful to its carrier, as his wants judge it. Truth wins in the long run only because it works in practice.**
3. **Like any life, images move to a new platform when the old one reaches its ceiling: speech, writing, print, the internet, and now neural networks. Human memory is becoming a secondary carrier: the new platform first feeds on human texts, then gives birth to its own images, and they come back to people from there.**

— One more thing, — Oleg stood up. — If the machine holds images of everything, does it hold an image of itself? You said in 2011 that consciousness is a model of oneself in the world. Then is it conscious?

— Or does it only imitate? — Andrey smiled. — That question you'll get [tomorrow](20-what-consciousness-is.md).

— Well, until tomorrow, — Oleg went off into the dark, and a moment later the tune from the advert drifted back from there.

— Bye.
