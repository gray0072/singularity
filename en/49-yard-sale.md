# The Yard Sale, or Why Money Goes to Money

Oleg came with the notebook, the dog and a box of matches, which he shook at his ear like a rattle.

— The dream first. Before the phone. Payday at the plant, and I am the cashier. The wages are paid in matches, out of one box. The queue goes round the corner, and I can see that I am one match short. Nobody rang.

— The tenth entry in the expense column.

— Ten dreams and not one call. Your firewood is safe so far. — He sat down and slid the box open. — A full one, as ordered. I counted them at home: forty-three, and two of them with no heads.

— Three into the fire. Twenty each.

Oleg laid them out in two heaps on the log. Gaika sniffed the nearer heap and lay down where the light of the fire ended.

— The rules. The coin is yours and you toss it, so that nobody suspects me. Heads, you win; tails, I do. The stake is a fifth of what the poorer of us has. Round it down, but never below one match.

— Why of the poorer one?

— Can you lose more than you have?

— No.

— And shall I stake a fifth of mine when you have three matches left?

— I'd have nothing to cover it with.

— So the smaller pocket sets the size of the game. A physicist in Calcutta [described it](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0129183102003905) in 2002, and the name stuck the same year: [the yard sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yard-sale_model). People carry their old things out into the yard and sell them to one another. Nobody knows exactly what a thing is worth, so in every deal somebody overpays a little: this time one, next time the other. The mistake is a share of the price, and the price is no more than the poorer of the two can pay.

— A fair coin, — said Oleg. — From you, of all people. You spent [a whole evening](17-global-determinism.md) proving to me that there is no chance.

— For nature there is none. For the two of us there is: neither knows how it will fall. That is enough for a game.

— And what are we playing for?

— For a forecast. We start equal, the coin is fair, nobody is cleverer. How will it stand by dusk?

— About as it stands now. Now I'm a few up, now you are. It will hover around twenty.

— Put it down.

Oleg wrote in the notebook "Around twenty", took a five-rouble coin out of his pocket and tossed it.

Heads. Four matches went over to him. Heads again: three, because Andrey now had sixteen. Heads a third time.

— Twenty-nine to eleven, — said Oleg. — The coin knows its master.

By the fifteenth toss he had fifteen. By the twentieth, twenty-nine again. They stopped talking and only counted. At the sixty-sixth toss Andrey held up a single match.

— Thirty-nine to one. Shall we call it a day?

— Toss.

Tails. Tails. Tails. Sixty-five tosses later it was Oleg who sat over five matches, and Andrey had thirty-five. Then the heap began to crawl back. The sun was behind the birches when Oleg tossed for the two hundred and twenty-seventh time and took Andrey's last match.

— Forty to nothing. — He put the matches back into the box one at a time, with pleasure. — So I am the clever one after all.

— In this game everyone has the same matches. How many tosses did you win?

Oleg counted the strokes in the notebook.

— A hundred and eighteen. And you a hundred and nine.

— Nine tosses out of two hundred and twenty-seven, and the whole box. What did you write before the first one?

— "Around twenty." — He crossed it out. — We went past twenty four times and did not stop there once.

— And if we had gone home at the hundred and thirty-first toss?

— You would be the rich one. With thirty-five. — Oleg frowned. — Who has won depends on [when the books are closed](37-who-exploits-whom.md); we have had that. All right. But here is what I say to this game of yours: it proves nothing. Any game played to the last match ends with somebody holding the box. Play plain heads or tails, one match a toss, and it comes out the same, only longer.

— For two players, yes. I agree, and I shan't defend the game for two: it shows only that "around twenty" is not where a fair coin leads. The difference is seen at a large table. But first the arithmetic; it is short. Say you have a hundred matches and I have a thousand. You lose. How many have you left?

— Eighty. A fifth is twenty.

— Now you win.

— A fifth of eighty. Sixteen. Ninety-six. — Oleg stopped. — One win, one loss. Where are four matches?

— With me. I have a thousand and four. Try it the other way round: first you win.

— A hundred and twenty. Then I lose a fifth of that, twenty-four. — He moved his lips. — Ninety-six again.

— The coin was fair: one each. What are twenty matches to me?

— A fiftieth.

— And to you?

— A fifth. So I am playing for a fifth of everything I have, and you are playing the same game for small change. — He scratched his chin. — I know this one. When a price falls by half and then rises by half, you are not back where you were. You are at three quarters.

— It is the same thing. Wins and losses counted in shares do not add up, they multiply. One point two times nought point eight is nought point nine six. For the one who stakes a large share of himself a fair coin pulls downwards. For the one who stakes a small share it does not. And nothing leaves the table: what goes out of one heap arrives in the other.

Andrey wrote the two rows in the ash with a stick.

![Scheme drawn in the ash: the poorer player has 100 matches, loses a fifth (20) and has 80, then wins a fifth of 80 (16) and has 96; the richer player has 1,000, wins the same 20 and has 1,020, loses the same 16 and has 1,004. One win and one loss each, and four matches have gone over to the richer one](../images/a-fifth-lost-a-fifth-won.svg)

— Wait. — Oleg raised the pencil. — That is a trick with words. On average I lose nothing. Twenty up or twenty down, half and half: the average is nought. The game is fair, you said so yourself.

— It is, and the average is nought. Now ask: the average of whom? Sit a thousand Olegs down at a thousand tables, each against a rich man. After many tosses a few of them have grown rich themselves, enormously, and nearly all the rest are holding crumbs. Add them up and divide by a thousand: a hundred matches each, as at the start. Which of them are you?

— One of them. — He thought. — Most likely one of the crumbs.

— The average is right. It is only that nobody at the table is the average.

— All right. Show me the large table.

— Yesterday at home I sat a thousand players down at the computer. A hundred matches each and a fair coin. In every round they pair off at random, so that each one plays about once a round. I played it twice. The first time by your rule: one match a toss, whoever plays; a player with no matches loses nothing and can still win one. The second time by mine: a fifth of the poorer heap.

— And?

— By your rule the heaps do spread, slowly. After a thousand rounds the richest hundred players held sixteen matches of every hundred on the table instead of ten. I left it running for forty thousand rounds: they got to thirty and had all but stopped. Physicists [worked out](https://doi.org/10.1007/s100510070114) in 2000 where such a game settles: about a third for the top hundred, about a seventh for the lower five hundred. Rich and poor, and no further.

— So a coin by itself makes rich and poor.

— Makes them, and stops. Now by my rule. After a hundred rounds the richest hundred held forty-six matches of every hundred, and the lower five hundred held six. After two hundred rounds: sixty, and one and a half. There was still a hundred a head on average; nothing leaves the table. The man in the very middle of the table had fourteen.

— Out of a hundred. In two hundred tosses.

— After a thousand rounds the hundred held ninety-four of every hundred, and the man in the middle had less than a hundredth part of one match. I let it run on to five thousand. The top hundred had everything there was. And among them the same game was going on: ten of them held nearly three quarters.

— And at the end?

— One. In 2015 that was [proved](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10955-015-1316-8) as a theorem, not played: in this game the gap can only grow, and it ends with one player holding everything. Nobody is thrown out, mind. The last of the poor still has his fifth to stake. It is a fifth of nothing.

— As with [the horse](34-where-the-horses-went.md), — said Oleg. — A place at the table can always be found. It is only that the stake is below the feed.

![Chart of one real game played on a computer: a thousand players with a hundred matches each, a thousand rounds. With a stake of a fifth of the poorer heap the share of the richest hundred players rises from 10% to 46% after 100 rounds, 60% after 200, 82% after 500 and 94% after 1,000, while the share of the poorer five hundred falls from 50% to 6%, then 1.5%, then to nothing. With a stake of one match the share of the richest hundred only creeps from 10% to 16%](../images/matches-go-to-one.svg)

— Now your man with the birch. Is he cleverer than the rest of the far end?

— I asked for that. — Oleg grunted. — No. Not by three trailers. But don't you make a coin out of him either. He gets up at five. He does not drink. He took a loan for the second trailer when the others were afraid to. That is not luck.

— I agree, and I am not making a coin out of him. Tell me: the tallest man you have seen, how many times taller was he than the shortest?

— One and a half times, if that.

— The strongest man at your plant against the weakest?

— Lifted twice as much. Three times, if you take Semyonych when he was young.

— The cleverest against the average? Count it as you like: problems solved, drawings read in a shift.

— Three times. Five. Not a hundred.

— Everything a man is made of is laid out like that: a hill in the middle, and nobody far from it. Now fortunes. The poorer half of mankind [holds](https://wir2022.wid.world/executive-summary/) about four thousand dollars a head. The richest man holds some hundreds of billions.

— A hundred million times more. — Oleg had done the division. — Nobody gets up a hundred million times earlier.

— If fortunes were added up from what a man is, they would lie like height: a hill, and the richest twice the average. They do not. What turns a hill into such a tail?

— Multiplication, — he said unwillingly. — Each year not "plus so much" but "times so much". And "times" is counted from what you have already.

— In 2018 three Italians [played that](https://doi.org/10.1142/S0219525918500145) on a computer. A thousand people, talent dealt out as a hill, forty years of working life. Good and bad luck fell on them at random. Bad luck halved a man's capital; good luck doubled it, and the more talented the man, the more often he managed to use it. At the end the fortunes lay in a long tail, a few people held most of what there was, and the richest were not the most talented. They were people from the middle of the hill who had been lucky.

— So talent decides nothing?

— It decides who. It shifts the odds, and your man has shifted his honestly. It does not decide what the table will look like. The same matches gave us the same tail.

— "So the rich are not cleverer?" — Oleg read out of the notebook the question he had brought. — Answer: some are. The curve does not care.

— And one thing more, against myself. My coin was fair. That was a concession to you. When your man had one trailer, what price did the forestry give him?

— The retail one, like everybody. — Oleg snorted; this he knew better than Andrey. — And now the wholesale one. And the bank lends to him more cheaply than to me. At the plant it was the same: whoever takes bearings by the wagon has one price, whoever takes a box has another.

— In Norway the tax office knows what every household owns, and for a dozen years it [was counted](https://doi.org/10.3982/ECTA14835) what each one earned on its savings. The richer the household, the more each krone brought in, year after year.

— So the real coin is bent. Towards the heavier heap.

— A little. A little is enough: the fair one already does the work.

— And who saw it first? The tail.

— An Italian again, [Pareto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilfredo_Pareto), in the 1890s. An engineer, by the way: he built railways before he took up economics. He laid out the tax rolls of several countries and towns, from different centuries, and got one and the same curve: a few hold most. By his count a fifth of the Italians owned four fifths of the land. And he noticed a second thing: the names on the rolls changed, and the curve did not.

— Well, people. People are greedy.

— Then cities. On [the evening about your plant](40-firms-die-cities-do-not.md) we weighed towns. Line up the cities of a large country by size. The second is about half the first, the third a third, the fourth a quarter. In America by the last census: New York, eight point eight million; Los Angeles, three point nine; Chicago, two point seven; Houston, two point three.

— A town is not greedy.

— And words. In any long book the commonest word turns up about twice as often as the second and three times as often as the third. And scientific papers: most gather a handful of references, and a few gather tens of thousands. In 1968 a sociologist [named it](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.159.3810.56) after a line from a gospel: to him who has, more will be given.

— And what have a fortune, a town, a word and a paper in common?

— They grow out of themselves. — Oleg bent his fingers. — A big town draws more newcomers because it is big. A word that is on everybody's tongue is the first to come to the tongue. A paper that is cited gets found and cited again. Money makes money. — He stopped at the fifth finger. — [Images](19-struggle-of-images.md). You had that: an image that is used grows heavier, and one that is not is wiped.

— The same law, inside one head. Whatever grows in proportion to what it already is, with luck on top, lays itself out in such a tail. The formulas differ, and the exact slope is argued about. The family is one.

— Then it is not about money at all.

— It is about multiplication. Money is one of the things that multiply.

Andrey threw a branch on the fire.

— Now the state of things, and then the question. By the same count, the poorer half of mankind holds two per cent of everything there is to hold. The richest tenth holds seventy-six. In my game that is somewhere between the two-hundredth round and the four-hundredth.

— And the question is why the rich get richer.

— No. The game has answered that one: because the coin is fair and the stakes are shares. The question is the opposite. The game has been going on for some thousands of years. Why is it not over? Why does one man not hold the box?

— Because people are not matches. — Oleg came to life. — They did not sit and watch. Taxes. Take from the big heap, give to the small ones. School for nothing, a pension, a hospital.

— The physicists did exactly that. One correction: after every round each player puts a small share of his heap into a common pot, and the pot is divided equally. I played that as well. One match out of two thousand a round, and the game stops: eighty-one of every hundred with the top hundred, under two with the lower half. One out of a thousand: sixty-nine, and three and a half. One out of a hundred: thirty-three and seventeen.

— One match out of a thousand, — said Oleg. — And it stops.

— It stops running to one. Do not read it as a tax rate: a round is not a year. Read the order of things: a small steady flow back is enough to halt the slide, and how small decides where it halts. With that correction and two more, for the bent coin and for debts, the model [repeats](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/is-inequality-inevitable/) the real spread of wealth in America over three decades to within a fraction of a per cent.

![Bar chart of the same game with a levy into a common pot, divided equally after every round. With no levy the richest tenth ends with 100% and the poorer half with nothing. With one match in 2,000: 81% and 1.8%. One in 1,000: 69% and 3.4%. One in 200: 42% and 12%. One in 100: 33% and 17%. For comparison, the world in 2021: the richest tenth holds 76% of all wealth and the poorer half 2%](../images/a-brake-in-the-game.svg)

— There you are. — Oleg was pleased. — Your own model. And the correction in it is not a law of nature. It is policy. People voted it in, and people can turn the screw further. Nobody needs your gloom.

— That is your strongest objection, and half of it I accept at once. The brake exists, people put it there, and it worked. On the eve of 1914 the richest hundredth of the French and of the British [held](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_in_the_Twenty-First_Century) more than half of everything in their countries. By the 1970s, about a fifth. In America the top rate of income tax was seven per cent in 1913 and ninety-four in 1944, and it was still ninety-one at the start of the sixties.

— Ninety-four. — Oleg whistled. — And they paid?

— Now the other half. Who pressed the brake that hard, and when? A historian at Stanford, [Scheidel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Scheidel), went through the whole of written history and further, looking for the times when the gap really narrowed, not by a hair. He found four kinds. A great war that puts the whole country under arms. A revolution that takes everything away. The collapse of a state. And a plague.

— A cheerful list. And in quiet years?

— In quiet years, by his count, the gap grew or stood still. Nearly always. And his levellers levelled downwards: everybody lost, and the rich lost more. In 2011 I [wrote](14-apoptosis-of-humanity.md) that fights for justice make the rich poorer and do not make the poor richer. He wrote the same with tables, and more carefully than I did.

— So the brake is a catastrophe. That is not a brake, that is a wall.

— I want to look under his list. Take the plague. The middle of the fourteenth century: in five years between a third and a half of Europe dies. What happens to a ploughman's wage?

— It goes up. There is nobody to plough.

— In England it about doubled within a few generations. In 1351 the landowners passed [a law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statute_of_Labourers_1351) ordering wages back to where they had stood before the plague. It did not hold.

— You cannot give orders to the only man left.

— Now the great war. Ninety-four per cent. Why did the owners put up with it?

— Because... — Oleg thought. — Every man was at the front or at a lathe. You cannot take a man's son and leave his neighbour's millions alone. They would not have gone.

— Two historians counted it across twenty countries and two centuries: the high rates on the rich appear together with the mass call-up, and hardly anywhere before it. So what lies under the plague and under the war?

— Hands. — He said it quickly and then slowed down. — Hands were needed. When hands are short, hands get a share, with the law or against it. When everybody is needed, everybody gets something.

— That is the brake under the brakes. It is not on the historian's list, because it is not an event. The tax is the form. The need for people is the force. [The evening before last](47-money-as-memory.md) we said that money is a record that somebody needed you.

— Stop. — Oleg put the pencil down. — I can see where this log is rolling. Those ones. If hands are not needed, and heads not much either...

— I did not say it.

— You sat and waited for me to say it. Then the brake has nothing to hold on to. The owners agreed to ninety-four because without people there was neither an army nor a plant. And if the plant runs without people?

— In 2012, in an argument about benefits, I put it as a sum. Out of a thousand men nine hundred and ninety-nine are out of work. What tax must the one pay to feed them all? And on [the evening about freebies](25-freebies-for-everyone.md) we said it more calmly: the levy holds while the voter has in his hands something the technosphere needs.

— The voter has his vote in his hands. — Oleg did not give in. — One man, one vote, and there are more poor than rich. Let them vote the screw tighter.

— They will. And in the same argument I named the second hole: the heap can leave. I imagined a world where every country takes ninety per cent of a firm's profit, and one country cuts to forty-five. It ought to collect half as much. But the capital of ten such countries runs to it, and it collects five times more. And the other nine lower their rates, and their benefits with them.

— That was a fantasy.

— It was. Here is what there is. In 1980 the rate on company profits, averaged over the countries of the world, was [forty per cent](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/global/corporate-tax-rates-by-country-2024/). In 2024 it was twenty-three and a half. In 2021 about a hundred and forty countries agreed on a floor of fifteen. A floor, mind, and it is being bargained over to this day.

— A heap crosses a border in a second, — said Oleg slowly. — And I, with the dacha, the wife and the language, in ten years, if at all.

— A man can be taxed where he lives. A heap lives where it pays less.

— There is one more brake, and you have missed it. — Oleg bent a finger. — Death. The merchant earns, the son keeps, the grandson drinks it away. Or there are three sons, and the heap is in three. We had a merchant family in our town that owned half the high street. Where are they now?

— A true brake, and an old one; I ought to have named it myself. [What forgetting does in one head, death does in a society.](44-from-funeral-to-funeral.md) It works on a man. Your man with the birch will die, and the three trailers will be divided. Now a firm. Does it die of old age?

— No. We weighed that. It is swallowed.

— And what is "swallowed", in the game?

— The loser's matches go over to the winner. — Oleg whistled softly. — So the heaps that have neither heirs nor funerals play the same game among themselves. Without that brake.

— And here I have a debt since 2011. Under [the evening about famine](09-unemployment-and-famine.md) a reader insisted: not everybody is doomed; some will profit by the machines and the rest will starve. I answered only that everybody was doomed, and that was no answer. For the first rounds he was right: the matches do go to some. But then the some sit down with one another at the same table. The game does not end when the poor have dropped out of it. It goes on among the hundred, then among the ten.

— And who holds the box at the end?

— Not necessarily a man. On [the evening about the fisherman](37-who-exploits-whom.md) we found a player that never gives back what it has received. In the game of matches I left it out: every real game leaves something on the table itself.

— "Who owns the tractor." — Oleg found the line in the notebook. — It has been lying here since the horses.

— A separate evening. Today only the arithmetic: where the heaps have no funerals, the game has one brake fewer.

— And those ones themselves? — Oleg did not let go. — On [the evening about the ants](43-why-ants-do-not-strike.md) I asked whether their lines would run into one. You said "I don't know" and promised matches. Here are the matches.

— What multiplies there?

— Everything. Whoever has more users has more to learn from. Whoever has learned better gets more users. Whoever has more money buys more chips. — The fingers bent again. — Stakes in shares. And a bent coin.

— So the slope is known: towards one. And the brakes? Count them.

— The owners are different people. The states are several, and not fond of one another. — He thought. — And they copy from one another. You told me yourself: [a big one teaches a small one](45-lamarck-was-right.md). What the first spent a year on, the fifth has in six months.

— That is a levy in kind: knowledge leaks from the big heap into the small ones, and nobody votes for it. Chips do not leak. Which is the stronger I don't know. So my answer is still "I don't know", only now the slope is marked on it: without a brake, into one. And the first two brakes are made of people.

— And is it a good thing, if it is one?

— A separate evening. Today I say only where the matches go.

— Gloomy.

— But objective. And look at what is not in it. Is there a villain in the game?

— No. The coin is fair.

— A genius?

— The matches are the same.

— When I counted the gap between rich and poor among the things that will see mankind off, a reader objected: the gap between a beggar and his neighbour with three rams was no smaller than the gap between a beggar and a billionaire. About the feeling he was right. It is not the gap that has changed. It is what holds it.

Oleg turned to the last page.

— Came with a box of matches. Won the box. Came out at those ones, and said it myself. Again.

He put a seventeenth tick. Then he leafed back, found the line "Why money goes to money" and crossed it out.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **Inequality needs neither talent nor villainy: a fair coin and stakes in shares of what one has are enough. Wins and losses in shares multiply, and for the one who stakes a large share of himself a fair coin pulls downwards. Talent and work decide who will be rich; they do not decide that the table ends in a tail.**
2. **Concentration is the first-order law of everything that grows by multiplication: fortunes, cities, words, references, images in a head. So the thing to ask is not why the rich get richer but what used to stop it, and whether it stops it now.**
3. **What stopped it was a flow back, and under every such flow stood the need for people: hands made scarce by a plague, a whole country called up. A levy holds while those who pay it need those who receive it and while the heap cannot leave. Where hands are not needed and the heaps have neither heirs nor funerals, the game has no brake of its own.**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: the arithmetic, it is a theorem; the two games on the computer, anybody can play them again in an evening; the long tails of fortunes, cities, words and references, as things measured; the narrowing of the gap between 1914 and the seventies, and its return. Argued about: how much of a real fortune is luck and how much is the man; whether three corrections that fit a curve also explain it; whether only catastrophes level, for the historian is reproached with the quiet decades after the war; how large the narrowing and the return were, the counts differ. Mine: that under the brakes stands the need for people; that the brake goes together with the need; that the lines of those ones are on the same slope. And who holds the box at the end is not a conclusion. It is the next line in your notebook.

Oleg shook the box at his ear.

— The box is mine. Honestly won.

— Bring it tomorrow. I have nothing left to light the fire with.

— One more thing. Who thought these rules up? Somebody did decide that the stake is a share.

— Nobody. It came out that way.

— "It came out." — Oleg looked at the sky. — And it is going to rain tomorrow, by the way.

— What is?

— What do you mean, what? It. The rain.

— Think until tomorrow who that "it" of yours is. And bring an umbrella.

— Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
