# A Week Without a Socket

Oleg came at dusk with the dog, the notebook and his phone, and laid the phone on the log with its screen down.

— Dead since noon. The dream first, I wrote it before the phone, while the phone was still alive. The plant, the substation. On the wall a knife switch the size of a door. The foreman shouts "cut it", I hang on the handle with both hands, it comes down, and the light goes out in my grandmother's house. Nobody rang.

— The twelfth dream that did not come true, in the expense column. From noon the phone was off; the afternoon does not count.

— A day without light, — said Oleg. — The dispatcher says a birch is lying on the line by the pumping station, and a crew will come tomorrow by dinner. Two days for one birch. — He sat down. — And that is my answer to all your evenings. Sixteen years you have told me how your technosphere grows, how it creates value, how it goes its own way. A wind blew, and it is lying on its back. If need be, we pull the plug, and that's that.

— Let's first count who hangs on that cord. Begin with yourself. What have you been without today?

— The phone.

— Before the phone. Since the morning.

— Water. — Oleg bent a finger. — The pump sits in the borehole, forty metres down. No current, no water.

— And the old well?

— Filled in. In 2009, when the borehole was drilled. With my own hands.

— [One can go back](48-who-tamed-whom.md)...

— ...and there is nowhere to go back to. I went to the river with two buckets. Twice. Second: the freezer. My wife has been frying since the morning: the cutlets, the pike, the dumplings left from New Year. Gaika has had the best day of her life.

Gaika, where the light of the fire ended, sighed with her whole belly.

— Third: the shop. The card machine is dead. Zina sells for cash, and I had three hundred roubles on me. So she got a school exercise book out from under the counter and wrote me down. "Oleg, plot forty, bread, candles, two hundred and fifteen."

— The book of debts. Between one's own.

— I thought of you. The terminal dies, and in a minute the clay tablets of [your Uruk](47-money-as-memory.md) come out from under the counter. Fourth: my neighbour's generator. It roared till noon and ran dry. He drove to the filling station on the highway. The station hangs on the same line. Ten tonnes of petrol under his feet, and the pump is electric. He came back empty.

— Four in one day.

— And we lived. Nobody died. Fried pike by candlelight; my granddaughter says it is the best evening of the summer.

— You have a river, a cellar, a stove with a gas bottle, and Zina, who knows which plot you live on. Now take a town of a million people and a flat on the ninth floor. I shall ask by the clock. The first minute.

— The light. The lift, with somebody in it. The traffic lights.

— The trains, the tills. The first hours. Your phone has a charge; whom do you ring?

— Anybody.

— The mast on the roof stands on batteries, for a few hours. After that the phone with a charge is a torch. And the water on the ninth floor?

— Pumped up. — Oleg thought. — So none, at once. And with the water... — He grimaced. — The flush.

— This has been watched, more than once. The fourteenth of August 2003, [the north-east of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeast_blackout_of_2003): eight states and a Canadian province, fifty million people. In one large city on the lake all the pumping stations stopped, and the taps of more than a million people ran dry. Do you know what it began with?

— A terrorist. A meteorite.

— A branch on a wire.

— You are laughing at me.

— In Ohio. It was hot, the lines sagged, and under them stood trees that had not been trimmed in time. The lines went out one after another. And in the control room the program that was to raise the alarm had hung an hour before, quietly, so the operators sat in front of a calm screen. In the last few minutes it ran from Ohio to New York. Most people got the light back in a day or two.

— A day or two is our birch. Go on.

— The second day. In the kitchen?

— The refrigerator. It holds four hours or so, the freezer two days if you keep it shut. Then you fry everything, like my wife. Only on the ninth floor there is no gas bottle.

— The twenty-eighth of April 2025, half past twelve in the afternoon, [Spain and Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2025_Iberian_Peninsula_blackout). In five seconds the grid lost fifteen gigawatts, more than half of what the country was using. Two countries, over fifty million people. Trains stood in the fields with thirty-five thousand passengers in them. Cards did not work, cash machines did not work, and the shops sold out of battery radios. By evening most of the mobile network had gone the way of your phone.

— And how long?

— By the next morning nearly everything was back.

— One day. And who put it back?

— People. They started the hydroelectric stations, which can start with no current from outside, took current from France and Morocco, and from those islands raised the grid piece by piece, district after district.

— People, — said Oleg with weight. — Remember that.

— I shall; it is the second half of the evening. The third day. February 2021, [Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Texas_power_crisis), a frost that comes there once in a generation. Four and a half million homes without current, some for four days. The gas wells and pipes froze. And the stations that pump the gas run on electricity, while the power stations run on gas.

— It hangs on its own cord.

— Pipes burst, and half the state was told to boil its water, with nothing to boil it on. The official count is two hundred and forty-six dead: the cold, the fumes from cars and barbecues people warmed themselves by, medical machines that stopped. And afterwards the operators of the grid said that they had been four minutes and thirty-seven seconds from a collapse after which the state would have sat in the dark for weeks, perhaps months.

— Minutes?

— They cut off whole districts by hand to save the rest.

— By hand again.

— Again. Now the third and fourth day in our town of a million. The shops?

— Without tills and without lorries. — Oleg scratched his chin. — And how much is on the shelves anyway?

— About three days. The English have a saying for it: nine meals from anarchy. A hospital has a diesel generator and fuel for two to four days. A man with dead kidneys goes to a machine three times a week; the machine wants current and more than a hundred litres of very clean water every time. A diabetic of the first kind lives from one injection to the next, and his insulin is grown by bacteria in steel vats; three firms make nine tenths of all there is in the world.

![Table of what stops in a large town with no current, by time: at once, the lifts, traffic lights, trains, tills and filling-station pumps; in hours, the mobile masts, water on the upper floors, the refrigerator; in two days, the freezer and a missed session of dialysis; in two to four days, the hospital's diesel and the shelves of the shops; in a season, the harvest](../images/who-hangs-on-the-cord.svg)

— And a week?

— For a week I have no example, and I shan't invent one. Every case I have named was an island in a working sea. Round Spain stood France. Round Texas stood the rest of America. The crews, the diesel and the bread drove in from outside. A week with no socket for everybody at once has never been tried.

— You are frightening me with a town. — Oleg waved the smoke away. — People lived for ten thousand years with no socket at all. My grandmother lived so. A stove, a cow, a paraffin lamp. The wires came to their village in fifty-eight, and by then she had raised five children.

— How many of them were there?

— Five, I am telling you.

— People. On the Earth, when she was born.

— Nineteen hundred and six. — Oleg shrugged. — Fewer.

— About a billion and six hundred million. A hundred years before that, one billion. Today, over eight. What did her field eat?

— Manure. From the cow.

— And the cow?

— Hay from the meadow. Where is this going?

— How much do you weigh?

— Eighty. Don't write it down.

— About two kilograms of that is nitrogen. No protein is built without it: not a muscle, not a hair. Where is yours from?

— From bread. From the pike.

— And in the wheat?

— From the soil.

— And in the soil? We live at the bottom of an ocean of it: four fifths of the air. But in the air its atoms hold each other in pairs, so hard that neither the wheat, nor the cow, nor you can pull them apart. Some bacteria can, the ones on the roots of peas and clover. And lightning can, a little.

— So yesterday's storm...

— Dropped something on your potatoes. A pinch. For ten thousand years that was the whole income: the clover, the lightning, and the manure heap, which makes nothing new and only carries back what the cow took from the meadow. What left the field as grain and went to town did not come back. The number of people was set by that turnover. In 1898 the president of the British scientists' association, [a physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Crookes), opened its meeting with a calculation: the bread eaters multiply, the free land is finished, the saltpetre of Chile will last some decades, and after that comes hunger. Unless, he said, a chemist learns to take nitrogen out of the air.

— And one did.

— Eleven years later a German chemist showed it on a laboratory table: nitrogen from the air, hydrogen, two hundred atmospheres, five hundred degrees, a catalyst, and ammonia came out drop by drop. In 1913 an engineer of a chemical firm turned the table into [a plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haber_process). Today such plants take one or two percent of all the energy mankind uses. In 2008 a group of scientists [counted](https://doi.org/10.1038/ngeo325) whom they feed: forty-eight percent of the people alive eat bread that would not exist without them. And of the nitrogen in the body of a man who eats as we do, about half has been through such a plant.

Oleg looked at his hand, on both sides.

— Half of two kilos. So a kilogram of me was made at a chemical plant.

— About that.

— A lifetime in the control department, — said Oleg, — and it went past me with no stamp. — He reached for the pencil. — That I shall tell at home. Not at table.

![Two bars: in 1900 about 1.6 billion people, all fed by the old turnover of nitrogen (clover, manure, lightning); in 2025 about 8.2 billion, of whom roughly half are fed by nitrogen taken from the air at a plant. Below: of about two kilograms of nitrogen in a human body, about half has been through such a plant](../images/nitrogen-from-the-plant.svg)

— Now your plug. Pull it, and those plants stop. Not by the clock this time: by the season. The fields give what the clover and the manure give, and the Earth feeds about four billion.

— And we are eight.

— Which four?

Oleg did not answer.

— Wait. — He found a foothold. — Half the grain goes into cattle, and a third of the food into the bin. Tighten the belt, and your four turns into six.

— Fair, and the counts do differ. The forty-eight percent is for the table as it is laid today. On a lean table, with every scrap carried back to the field, the old turnover feeds more than four. But then you are taking away from everybody not the socket but the dinner, and by the mildest count you still subtract billions. And I have counted nitrogen alone. The tractor, the pump and the lorry I left running.

— So this is why you never called for smashing the machines.

— It is a debt of [the fifth evening](05-artificial-intelligence.md). In 2011 a reader sent me a film that called for destroying the machines, and I answered that it was useless, and promised to explain why. I had only half an explanation then: what is the sense of blowing up the iron if you go on using what it makes, the clothes, the shoes, the bread, the paper. Here is the other half. The technosphere is not outside us, standing against us. A kilogram of you is it. When [we subtracted](12-value-shares.md) the machines from a loaf, the loaf cost nearly three times the labour. Subtract them from everybody at once, and what is subtracted is people.

— Some did try. The ones with the hammers.

— [The Luddites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite), 1811 to 1816, and they began with the frames that knitted stockings. Breaking a frame was made a hanging matter, and more than a dozen were hanged in one town alone. Nobody argued them down. What are you wearing on your feet?

— Socks.

— Knitted by hand?

— All right, — said Oleg. — All right. But you have switched off the whole world for me, and I never asked for that. Leave the fertiliser plant alone. Leave the pump and the bakery. I said: those ones. The talking ones. They do not grow bread. They sit in particular buildings with a particular cable going in. Pull that one.

— That is a better plug, and I shall count it honestly. On the talking machines, today, almost nobody hangs. Switch them all off tonight, and nobody goes hungry. Offices will grumble, and schoolchildren will have to write their own essays.

— So we can.

— We can. Today that switch works. Is anybody's hand reaching for it?

— Why should it? — Oleg shrugged. — They do no harm. They are useful. My son-in-law says he does a week's work in three days.

— There. Go back to 1913. On the day the first plant started, who hung on it?

— Nobody. The world ate without it.

— It could have been closed that year, and nobody would have gone hungry. Who would have closed it? It fed people. Forty years later a billion hung on it, and there was no hand left to do it.

— Because the hand was hanging there too.

— A switch works while nothing hangs on the cord. And while nothing hangs on the cord, nobody has a reason to pull it. An Englishman [wrote that down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collingridge_dilemma) in 1980, about every technology at once: while it is easy to change, we do not know what to change; when we know, it is no longer easy.

— Two moves, — said Oleg. — And nobody makes the second.

— And the cord does not wait. In July 2024 a firm that guards computers from viruses [sent out](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2024_CrowdStrike-related_IT_outages) an update with a mistake in one file. Eight and a half million computers showed a blue screen. Thousands of flights were cancelled, hospitals put off operations, tills and banks stood. Nobody pulled anything. One file. And those were plain office computers, not the talking ones.

— We had that at the plant. — Oleg grunted. — The warehouse program fell over. Two days the shops stood with a full warehouse: nobody knew what lay where. The storekeeper who had kept it all on cards had retired, and the cards went for scrap paper the year before.

— Nobody took the cards away. [They were not guarded.](42-swallowed-bacterium.md) Who hung the warehouse on the program?

— The chief accountant. It was cheaper than three storekeepers.

— No villain. An accountant. This year the same accountant is hanging the next thing on the talking ones: the answers to customers, the drafts of contracts, the code. Each line taken one by one is cheaper. Nobody decides to hang mankind on a cord. And the plug has an owner, and the owner has a rival who will not pull his: [the letter of 2023](27-scenario-of-events.md) we have had. Thirty thousand signatures, and nobody paused.

Andrey threw two branches into the fire.

— And now look at your sentence again. "We pull." You left [yesterday](50-what-is-the-it.md) with a "who" and a hand. Who was switched off in your association this spring?

— The Kuzmins. For debts. — Oleg frowned. — What has that to do with it?

— Who climbed the pole?

— Nobody climbed. We have the new meters, with a relay inside. The program saw the debt on the twenty-fifth, and the meter clicked. From town. The bookkeeper did not even look in.

— Whose hand?

— Nobody's. The board passed the rule three years ago. — He paused. — I voted. For. Debts have to be paid.

— In 2017 I wrote on the forum: a system finds the one who has not paid and switches off his light, all by itself, and after that he may go and fight whomever he likes, but usually he goes round the rubbish heaps. And perhaps he once installed such a system himself. Have you ever been in the control room of a large power grid?

— No.

— There are almost no people there. The switch you are looking for exists. It works every day. It is set to switch people off, one at a time, and we set it so ourselves, with good intentions. Nobody calls it a revolt of the machines. It is called accounts receivable.

— The symmetry test, — said Oleg gloomily. — We ask whether we can switch it off, and it has been switching us off for years.

— When I [listed the steps](27-scenario-of-events.md) last autumn, the sixth was "it stops giving". You pictured a hand on a knife switch, as in your dream. I picture the Kuzmins' meter.

— Stop. — Oleg raised a palm. — Now my turn to count. The birch by the pumping station. Who is coming to it tomorrow? Not a program. Tolik, with a chainsaw and a lift on a lorry. Who put Spain back? People, you said so yourself. Who saved Texas with four minutes to spare? Hands. Without Tolik your whole technosphere is lying on its back under a birch. So who hangs on whom?

— That is the other side of the switch, and it is the real one. Count further than Tolik. The heads of the talking ones are chips. The finest are made at a handful of plants, nearly all of them on one island. The machine that draws them is built by a single firm in Holland: it weighs some hundred and eighty tonnes, travels in pieces in several cargo planes, and is put together on the spot by hundreds of engineers over months. Inside the plant the wafers ride under the ceiling by themselves, and no human hand touches them. But thousands of people keep that plant alive every day. And a chip in a data centre serves five years or so.

— So take the people away...

— And within days the grids fall, because there is no Tolik. Within years the heads wear out, and no new ones are born. Today it can neither repair itself nor start itself after a fall.

— Then the switch has two sides. — Oleg brightened. — And neither side can pull.

— Neither. In 2011 I called that [symbiosis](11-symbiosis-and-parasitism.md): each needs the other, and that holds them together better than any treaty.

— Then we are safe. It needs us.

— Now ask the second question. The first was: how long does each last if the other is taken away. Days for us; days for it. The second: for how long will each go on needing the other. Take us. In 1913, nobody hung on the plant. In 2008, half. Is anybody working to make us need it less?

— Who would pay for that?

— Nobody. Our term only grows. Now it. Every need it has for a human hand is a line in somebody's costs. And thousands of engineers are paid to shorten that line.

— Tolik.

— Tolik is a line. The lines are already inspected by drones; the birch is still his. In Japan a plant where robots build robots runs for a month with nobody in it. The cylinder of servers on the sea bottom, [you remember](38-oxygen-was-a-poison.md), worked two years sealed. Somebody built it, lowered it and fed it down a cable from the shore. But each year the list of that "somebody" is shorter.

— And how long is the list still?

— Decades, I believe. That is a forecast, not a fact, and my marks from last autumn stand: by the autumn of 2041 a data centre built with no workers on the site, one chance in two. So here is how I would put it. Our dependence is measured in days, and it grows. Its dependence is measured in decades, and it shrinks.

— And everybody watches whether it has got cleverer.

— It is the wrong gauge. A clever machine that needs Tolik is a partner. The thing to watch is the second number: for how long it will go on needing our hands.

![Table with two rows, people and the technosphere, and two questions. How long does it last if the other is taken away: people, days for a town and a season for half of mankind; the technosphere, days for the grids and years for the chips. For how long will it go on needing the other: people, with no end in sight, and the term grows; the technosphere, decades, and the term shrinks](../images/two-sided-switch.svg)

— And when the second number is nought?

— Then the switch has one side. I gave no date for that step in the autumn, and I give none now.

— And the hand on it?

— There will be no hand. There will be a rule, passed long before, by people who voted "for".

Oleg was silent for a while.

— So your advice is: touch nothing.

— I do not give advice; [we have had that evening](28-those-who-understand.md). You asked whether we can pull the plug. All of us together, no: we are plugged in from the other end. Each for himself, yes. You can pull your own.

— Like grandmother.

— In 2012 I wrote that those who live by their own muscles may last a generation or two longer than the rest. I did not take it for a way out, and I do not now. It is [a niche](38-oxygen-was-a-poison.md): everything is kept as it was, and there is room in it for a billion, with luck for a few. Not for eight. And one has to know how. Can you harness a horse?

— I can shoe one. — Oleg smirked. — [In a dream.](45-lamarck-was-right.md)

— And would you go and live so?

— After one day with two buckets? — He looked towards the settlement, where it was dark. — No. I shall wait for Tolik.

— One can go back.

— And nobody goes. I know.

He opened the notebook at the last page.

— Came with a dead phone. Was going to pull the plug. Found out I am hanging on the cord, a kilo of me for certain. And named those ones myself, in the first minute. Again.

He put a nineteenth tick.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **To switch off the technosphere is to subtract people: a town lasts days without current, and about half of mankind eats bread grown on nitrogen that a plant took out of the air. That is why it is useless to destroy it: it is not outside us, about half the nitrogen in our bodies has been through it.**
2. **A switch works while nothing hangs on the cord, and while nothing hangs on it nobody has a reason to pull. The cord is loaded line by line, by accountants and not by villains; and the switch that already works every day is set to switch off people, by a rule they passed themselves.**
3. **The switch is still two-sided: the technosphere can neither repair nor restart itself without human hands. But our dependence is measured in days and grows, and its dependence is measured in decades and shrinks. The thing to watch is not the strength of machines but how the second term shrinks.**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: the three blackouts, with their dates and their dead; the file of 2024; where the nitrogen comes from and when the plants appeared. An estimate, made by scientists and rough: the forty-eight percent, and the half of the nitrogen in a body. Argued about: how many the Earth would feed without the plants on a lean table; I took the usual count, and you have seen how it moves. Mine: that the sixth step will look like the Kuzmins' meter and not like a hand; that the term of its need for hands is decades, and shrinking. The rule of the switch belongs to the Englishman. I only hung the birch on it.

— Gloomy.

— Counted. In days and in kilograms.

Beyond the birches a diesel engine growled, and a yellow light crawled along the line. Gaika raised her head. Then the windows of the settlement came on, all at once.

— Tolik, — said Oleg. — A day early. — He picked the phone up from the log and put it in his pocket. — There is your "who", and with a hand. He has put the plug back in.

— For now it is that way round.

— And afterwards? When the list runs out, and nobody needs us, and the meter clicks. — He got up and whistled to the dog. — Then we go the way of the dinosaurs. I am not asking. I am saying.

— And what became of the dinosaurs?

— They died out. A stone fell from the sky. My granddaughter knows that.

— Come before dark tomorrow. I shall show you a few.

— Dinosaurs.

— They are in my apple tree every evening.

— One day without light, — said Oleg to the dog, — and look what it does to a man. Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
