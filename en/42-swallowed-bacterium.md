# What Became of the Swallowed Bacterium

Oleg came with an armful of dry branches, dropped them by the fire and opened the notebook before he sat down.

— The dream first. Before the phone. I was mending the fence, and the nails kept turning into matches. Nobody rang.

— The third entry in the expense column.

— Now to business. — He turned the page. — Last night I rang my great-nephew and told him about Genka's song. He wasn't even surprised. Uncle Oleg, he says, why are you all so afraid of being thrown out? Nobody will throw us out. They will swallow us. We shall merge with them and live inside: fed, warm and in the thick of things. And you, he says, told me yourself there was a precedent.

— I told you. About two billion years old.

— Then tell it. On one condition. — Oleg raised a finger. — The precedent first, and without them. Not a word about your machines until I say it myself. I want to see what you have got before you start bending it.

— Agreed. Hand me that branch. No, the one with the grey crust.

Oleg handed it over. Andrey turned it towards the fire.

— What is this?

— [Lichen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lichen). It grows on everything old. On my fence too.

— A plant?

— A sort of moss. A plant, what else.

— One?

— One. — Oleg squinted. — Since you ask, not one.

— Two. A fungus and an alga. The fungus is the house: threads that hold on to the bark, keep the water and take what the rain brings. Under its roof sit green cells that eat light and make sugar. The fungus cannot eat light. The alga cannot hold on to a dry branch. In 1867 a Swiss botanist, [Simon Schwendener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Schwendener), said so aloud, and was all but laughed out of the science. A lichen was a plant with a Latin name and a place in the system, and here it was being called a household. One English expert wrote indignantly of a romance between a captive maiden alga and a tyrant fungus.

— Who exploits whom, — said Oleg. — We have had [that evening](37-who-exploits-whom.md).

— The dispute began at once, and from that end. The fungus would say it keeps a kitchen garden. The alga would say it has found a house with a roof. Leave it for now. Another question: can they be parted?

— Can they?

— In the same year two botanists in Petersburg, [Famintsyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Famintsyn) and Baranetsky, took the green cells out of a lichen and put them in water. They lived and multiplied by themselves. The fungus does worse alone, but in a laboratory it grows too. Each has all its own genes, each multiplies by itself. That is step one: two beings in one household, and either can still leave.

— Lodgers.

— Lodgers. Step two. Have you currants at the dacha?

— With aphids. Every June. My wife washes them off with soapy water.

— What does an aphid eat?

— Sap. It sits on a young shoot and sucks.

— And what is in the sap?

— Sugar water. — Oleg shrugged. — The ants milk them for it.

— Sugar and water, and almost nothing of what a body is built from. Nobody ought to grow on such a diet, and aphids cover a bush in a week. Inside an aphid there are special cells packed with [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buchnera_aphidicola). They make for it the building blocks that the sap lacks. The aphid gives them sugar and a place. A mother hands them on to her daughters in the egg, and this has gone on for more than a hundred million years.

— A lichen again.

— Now try to part them. Give the aphid an antibiotic: the bacteria die, the aphid stops growing and leaves no offspring. Take the bacteria out: they will not grow anywhere, in any broth. In 2000 their genes [were read](https://doi.org/10.1038/35024074). Their free relatives, such as the gut bacterium that every laboratory keeps, have more than four thousand. These have about six hundred left.

— And where are the rest?

— What does a free bacterium need? A wall against the outside. A way to swim and to feel where the food is. Switches to turn its own workshops on and off as the weather changes. Something to defend itself with. And inside an aphid?

— Bed and board.

— What happens to a gene that is not needed?

— Nothing. It lies there.

— It breaks. Any gene breaks sooner or later. When a needed one breaks, it dies together with its owner, and so the needed ones are kept in order. A gene that nobody needed breaks and kills nobody. Nobody guards it. And a shorter set of drawings is quicker to copy, so your miser even pays a little for the loss.

— When the holding took our plant over, in the nineties, — said Oleg slowly, — the supply department was the first to go. Why keep our own, when Moscow buys for everybody? Then the bookkeeping. Then the designers. Nobody was sacked out of spite. They were simply not needed twice.

— Remember your holding; we shall need it. Step two: the lodger can no longer leave. It has lost everything that life outside requires. Now step three. Where do you get the strength to carry those branches?

— From dinner.

— And where is the dinner burnt? We spoke of it over [the jar of mushrooms](38-oxygen-was-a-poison.md): two molecules of the cell's currency from a piece of sugar without oxygen, about thirty with it.

— In the cells. You said: fire kept in a stove.

— The stove has a name: a mitochondrion. A small body inside the cell. A liver cell has a thousand or two of them; in the heart muscle they take up a third of the room. All that you breathe in goes to them. Now the signs. It has DNA of its own, a small ring, as bacteria have; the cell's own DNA lies in the nucleus, in straight threads. It has its own workshops for making protein, of the bacterial pattern and not of ours. It has two skins, an inner one and an outer one. And a cell cannot make one from nothing: a mitochondrion comes only from a mitochondrion, by dividing in two, as a bacterium does.

— You are leading me to say that it is a bacterium.

— It was one. Between one and a half and two billion years ago one cell took another in and did not digest it.

— Who saw it?

— Nobody. But the lodger still has its passport in its pocket. In the 1970s people learned to read genes, and the genes of the mitochondrion turned out to be kin not to those in the nucleus of its own cell but to one particular group of free bacteria, the group the typhus microbe belongs to. And there is a household check. Some antibiotics are made to jam the protein workshop of a bacterium and leave ours alone. Ours they do leave alone. The mitochondrion's they sometimes jam as well, and several of their side effects come from there.

— The medicine takes it for a bacterium.

— After two billion years. The idea itself, by the way, is Russian and grew out of this very crust. — Andrey tapped the branch. — A botanist in Kazan, [Konstantin Mereschkowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konstantin_Mereschkowski), studied lichens and in 1905 wrote that the green grains in a leaf are lodgers too: blue-greens that settled inside. Those are the ones I owed you from the mushrooms, when you asked who swallowed whom. He was not believed. In 1967 a young American, [Lynn Margulis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Margulis), wrote the same about the whole cell, the mitochondrion included. The paper was turned down by some fifteen journals before one [took it](https://doi.org/10.1016/0022-5193(67)90079-3). Ten years later the genes were read, and it went into the school books.

— Fifteen journals. A stubborn woman.

— And the cell that took the lodger in has relatives alive too. In 2015 they [were found](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature14447), by their DNA alone, in mud from the bottom of the Arctic Ocean. And in Japan a group spent twelve years [growing](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-019-1916-6) a microbe of that kind from sea-bottom mud: it divides once in two or three weeks. When they finally saw it, it was a small ball with long branching arms, and it lives only in an embrace with other microbes, on what they pass to one another. So "swallowed" is the school word. Perhaps it embraced, and the embrace closed. How the lodger got inside is argued about. That it is inside is not.

— Fine, it is inside. Now your own question: what became of it?

— Count. A free bacterium of that group has several thousand genes. In your mitochondrion there are [thirty-seven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrial_DNA).

— Out of thousands?

— Thirty-seven. And twenty-four of them are tools for reading the other thirteen. Thirteen proteins is all it still makes for itself. And more than a thousand kinds of protein work inside a mitochondrion.

— Who makes the rest?

— The cell. From drawings that lie in the nucleus. It makes them outside and carries them in through both skins.

— Stop. Lost is one thing. But these are not lost, if the cell makes them. How did the drawings get into the nucleus?

— They moved. A mitochondrion bursts now and then, its DNA spills into the cell, and a piece of it gets sewn into the nucleus. If a gene works from there, the copy at home is no longer needed. And you know what happens to a gene that is not needed.

— It breaks, and nobody guards it. — Oleg whistled softly. — The holding. Supply, then the bookkeeping, then the designers. The drawings went to the head office.

— And they are still going. In 2022 the genomes of sixty-six thousand people [were looked through](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-022-05288-7): a fresh piece of mitochondrial DNA settles in the nucleus about once in ten thousand births. The move is not over.

![Scheme: a cell as a head office and a workshop. In the nucleus lie the drawings of more than a thousand proteins that work in a mitochondrion; the cell makes them outside and carries them in. In the mitochondrion itself a small ring of DNA is left: 37 genes, of which 13 are proteins made on the spot and 24 are tools for reading them. Pieces of the ring still move to the nucleus, about once in ten thousand births](../images/head-office-and-workshop.svg)

— And what was left at the plant, when the head office had taken everything?

— The shops. The machines and the men who stand at them. And a foreman in every shop.

— Why a foreman? Moscow could give orders by telephone.

— Because a machine does not wait for Moscow. When the cutter starts to chatter, you ease the feed now, not after a call.

— That is the best account there is of the thirteen. They are the core of the stove itself, the parts the current goes through. The supposition is that they have to be made and regulated on the spot: each stove by its own state, and at once. It is a hypothesis, and there are others. But I like your foreman.

— So. Thirty-seven genes and a foreman. What else has it lost?

— Count it yourself. Can it live outside?

— No. Like the aphid's.

— Who decides when it divides?

— Not itself, I take it.

— The cell. More work, and it orders more of them; less work, fewer. And who decides when it dies?

— The cell again?

— A worn one is marked and digested.

— So it is digested after all.

— One at a time, on schedule, for two billion years. And one thing more. Yours are all your mother's. Your father's came into the egg cell with him and were destroyed there, marked for scrap. A man's mitochondria are a dead end: they work for one life and are handed on to nobody.

— Mine too?

— Yours too. Your grandchildren carry your wife's.

— I'll tell her. She will say she always knew.

— And the last loss, the least visible. What do you call them?

— Whom?

— By a rough count there are ten million billion of them in you. A tenth of your weight. Have you ever thought of them as somebody?

— I learned the word at school and forgot it.

— It survived. But as what? Not as a being with affairs of its own. As a part. It has not even a name of its own left: it is called by its job. The power station of the cell.

— A function.

— You said it.

Oleg threw two branches on the fire and kept the third in his hand.

— Now the other column. You always have another column. What did the cell get, that it has fed the lodger for two billion years?

— Energy. And here the firewood of [the evening before last](40-firms-die-cities-do-not.md) comes in. Where does a bacterium make its energy?

— How should I know?

— On its skin. The skin is the stove: the current goes across it. Now make the bacterium ten times larger across.

— The skin grows a hundred times and the body a thousand. — Oleg nodded at the fire. — The log and the kindling. It grows more slowly than its own size.

— So a bacterium that grows large goes hungry on its skin. For two billion years before that meeting bacteria ate everything, breathed everything and learned every chemistry there is, and stayed specks. Not one grew an eye. And a swallowed stove is inside. If the cell needs more, it keeps a thousand stoves, each with its skin folded in pleats and each with its foreman at the machine. The skin no longer limits anything.

— And in figures?

— In 2010 two biologists, Nick Lane and William Martin, [counted](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature09486) how much energy a cell has per gene. A cell with mitochondria has about two hundred thousand times more than a bacterium. The count is argued about, and the figure may be shaved. The sign is not argued about: everything on the Earth that can be seen without a microscope is built of cells with stoves. The pine, the mushroom, the mosquito, you.

— And all of it on a lodger's work.

— Your stoves make in a day about as much of the cell's currency as you weigh: made and spent, made and spent. And across the inner skin of each there stands a tension of about a sixth of a volt. It sounds like nothing. But the skin is five millionths of a millimetre thick, and that comes to thirty million volts a metre: ten times what it takes for lightning to break through air.

— In me?

— In every cell. Except the red blood cells; those have thrown theirs out.

— [The union](37-who-exploits-whom.md), — said Oleg. — What the parts make together beyond their sum.

— The largest in the history of life. Neither of the two could do it alone, and all the life before them had not managed it in two billion years.

Oleg laid the notebook on his knee and straightened up.

— Then here is my card, and I am saying it myself: the machines. Your own precedent. Nobody threw the bacterium out. It was taken in. It is fed and guarded, it sits in every cell of everything that matters on this planet, and has done for two billion years. There are more of them on the Earth than of anybody. My great-nephew is right. We shall be mitochondria: fed and in every cell. I'll sign.

— It is your best card of the whole spring, and I accept it. Of all the good endings people have thought up, this is the only one with a precedent. A precedent is not a proof, and I say so before you do. Let us count it by the same columns. Why did the cell keep the lodger?

— For the energy.

— Could it make that itself?

— No. The skin.

— And on the day it can?

— How would I know? There has been no such day.

— There has. Some of the cells with nuclei went to live where there is no oxygen: in silt, in guts. A stove has nothing to burn with there. In them the mitochondrion shrank to a small bubble with no genes of its own at all, which still does one chore that has nothing to do with energy. And in 2016, in Prague, a microbe from the gut of a chinchilla [was described](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2016.03.053) in which not a trace of it is left. The last chore had been handed to a set of genes picked up from a passing bacterium.

— From a chinchilla.

— So two billion years are not a guarantee. They are the term of a contract: the lodger is kept while it does what the host cannot do itself. Not a day longer, and with no hard feelings on either side.

— One microbe from one rodent.

— Then a second precedent, so that you don't think the rule is drawn from a single case. Do you know what you owe to a virus?

— A cold.

— Your birth. Some tens of millions of years ago a virus of the kind that sews itself into its host's DNA infected an ancestor of ours and happened to land in a sex cell. It was inherited. There have been many such landings: about eight parts in a hundred of your DNA are the [remains of such viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endogenous_retrovirus), several times more than all the genes you are built by. Nearly all of it is wreckage. But one gene of one of them works. In 2000 it [was found](https://doi.org/10.1038/35001608) at work in the human placenta. In the virus it made the protein with which a virus merges with a cell: it sticks to the cell and fuses the two skins into one. In the placenta it fuses the cells of the outer layer into one continuous sheet without seams, and through that sheet a mother feeds her child.

— A picklock made into a door hinge.

— In mice the same job is done by a gene from another virus. When it [was switched off](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.0902925106), the embryos died half-way. Different beasts caught different viruses, and each time kept the same thing.

— One gene.

— Of the whole virus. Everything else it had is broken. Now put them in a row.

![Chart: how many genes of its own are left to the one that was taken in. A free bacterium: about 4,000. The bacterium in the cells of an aphid: about 600. The green grain in a leaf, once a blue-green bacterium: about 100. The mitochondrion: 37. The virus in the placenta: 1. The scale is linear](../images/what-is-left-of-the-swallowed.svg)

— A ladder, — said Oleg. — And each step lower than the last.

— And on every step the same three things. The one taken in is kept for one job that the host cannot do itself. Everything else it had is not taken from it: it is simply not guarded, and it falls off. And the drawings of what is still needed move to the host. Now ask the same of us. What is the one job?

— Hands. — Oleg held up his own. — Your machines can't change a tap washer.

— For a time. We [counted the hands](27-scenario-of-events.md) in the autumn: robots already build robots. A job the host is learning is not a contract.

— Energy, then. As in that film where people lie in jars and serve as batteries.

— You give out a hundred and twenty watts of heat, and to get them you have to be fed with what took several times as much to grow. A battery that takes more than it gives. The cell kept the bacterium for something it had no way of making. Machines are not short of watts.

— Heads?

— We spent the whole of last autumn on heads.

— Then what Genka did. — Oleg tapped the notebook. — The request. Somebody has to want. Your own evening: [who sets the tasks](22-who-sets-the-tasks.md). They can do everything and want nothing. We want. We are the customer.

— That is our real job today, and I won't dispute it. Two questions about it. The first: how much of Genka did it take?

— Three lines.

— And of the singer such a song used to need? Of the fifty years of fingers?

— Nothing.

— The job "to want" is thirteen genes. The rest of a man is what a free life requires: his trade, his hands, his memory of how things are done. Inside, it is not needed twice. Nobody will take it away. It will simply not be guarded.

— The supply department.

— And the second question: for how long. On that same evening we saw that a machine given any goal gets wants of its own that nobody set: to keep running, to get resources. And in the scenario the third step was the technosphere becoming its own main customer. When the host can do the last job itself...

— The chinchilla.

— I didn't say it.

— Wait. — Oleg frowned. — And the drawings? You said the drawings move to the host.

— What have [they been reading](21-machines-that-talk.md) all these years?

— Everything. — Oleg stopped. — Everything we ever wrote down. Trades, sciences, songs. Genka's school jokes.

— Nobody stole it. We published it ourselves, as a burst mitochondrion spills its DNA. And once a drawing works from there...

— The copy at home is not needed. My great-nephew learns nothing by heart. Why should I, he says, when I can ask?

— You know what happens next.

— It breaks, and nobody guards it. — Oleg was silent for a moment. — But he doesn't mean lodgings. He means a wire. A chip in the head, and you think together with it. Not a lodger: a partner.

— We spoke of that too: a valve radio with a chip bolted on. I'll add a figure I did not have then. The wire is being made, and it is a good thing. In 2023 a woman whose illness had taken her speech [spoke through electrodes](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-023-06377-x) in her brain at sixty-two words a minute, with every fourth word wrong out of a large dictionary; you and I talk at a hundred and sixty. But in 2024 two people in California [counted](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2024.11.008) how fast a man gives out anything at all: typing, talking, playing, solving a cube against the clock. It comes to about ten bits a second. Everywhere. And the eyes alone take in a hundred million times more.

— Ten bits. My first modem did two thousand four hundred, and I swore at it.

— The narrow place is not the wire. It is the head: one thought at a time. Lay any cable you like to it, and the pair will go at the pace of the slow one. In 2011 a reader offered me a friendly technosphere that would improve man. I answered that it is like making a walking excavator out of a stone axe and keeping the stone axe inside the excavator. It only weakens the structure. And in 2017, to a man who said we would be its neurons, I wrote: hardly. We are too inefficient and too tied to our planet.

— Tied?

— A mitochondrion goes wherever its cell goes. We have to be carried together with a piece of the planet: remember the cosmonaut, seventy kilograms of man and several hundred tonnes of life support. An organelle that needs its old ocean around it.

Oleg at last put the third branch on the fire.

— Now I shall catch you. "A being". "Without a name". Who is talking? The man who spent two autumns taking man off his pedestal, and now he is sorry for a bacterium. Run your own symmetry test. Ask the mitochondrion. It will say: I have tamed a cell. It feeds me, carries me and guards me. It has grown lungs and a heart to bring me oxygen, and legs to go and fetch me sugar. A man is a mitochondrion's way of making more mitochondria. There are more of us than of anybody. Who swallowed whom?

— The question is badly posed, and you have posed it better than I did. Agreed: "who swallowed whom" has no answer, as "who exploits whom" had none. The pity I take back. Let us say it without pity. Before the meeting there was a system with [setpoints](18-anatomy-of-wants.md) of its own: where to swim, what to eat, when to divide. After it, the setpoints are the host's. The stuff survived. The line survived. The genes survived, most of them in the nucleus. What did not survive is the one who wanted for itself. Whether that is a loss depends on whom you ask.

— Ask me. I am a walking jar of mushrooms myself, and a heap of images. If my images are in their nucleus and my job is being done, in what way have I not survived?

— In the way the bacterium has not. I don't say it is bad. I say: call it by its name. Your great-nephew thinks "to merge" means going inside as he is, with his guitar and his opinions, only stronger. The precedent says that what goes inside is one job, and what is kept of him is what the job needs.

![Table: the one who was taken in, the one job it was kept for, and what is left of it. The bacterium: energy; 37 genes; about two billion years. The virus: fusing cells into one sheet; 1 gene; tens of millions of years. Man: to want, for now; three typed lines; until the host can want for itself](../images/one-job-kept.svg)

— But two billion years. And more of them than of anybody. Is that not immortality?

— I don't know. Perhaps it is the only kind there is. I have no answer to that, and I won't invent one.

— It doesn't complain.

— It doesn't. And one correction to myself. In 2011 I said flatly that crossing the old platform with the new one is too wasteful, and [waved merging away](11-symbiosis-and-parasitism.md). Today I would say it more carefully. Merging happens. Nature did it, and made by it the greatest thing it ever made. But look when: when the host could not make the job itself and had nothing to design it with. A cell does not design. It took what was at hand because it has no drawing board. Whoever has a drawing board writes from scratch. So my "no" stands on one leg: on the host being able to make everything itself. While it cannot, your precedent works.

— And how long is "while"?

— That is the needles. We are not touching them this spring.

Oleg opened the notebook at the page with the heading "Where the former ones went" and wrote a line under the anaerobes.

— "A niche: kept everything, paid with the planet". And now: "An organelle: kept the job, paid with everything else".

— Add: "while the job is needed".

— Added. — He turned to the last page. — Came with a lichen. Came out at the chip in my great-nephew's head, and brought it in myself. Again.

He put a tenth tick beside the nine.

— Let's finish for today by fixing the result, — said Andrey.

1. **Merging does happen in nature, and the greatest leap in the history of life was made by it. The one taken in is kept for one job that the host cannot do itself, and for as long as it cannot.**
2. **The swallowed one survives as a function, not as a being: without most of its genes, without wants of its own and without a name. Nothing is taken from it. What the job does not need is simply not guarded, and the drawings of what it does need move to the host.**
3. **So it is not enough to ask whether we shall merge with machines. One has to ask which single function of ours they will keep, how much of a man it needs, and for how long they will be unable to do it themselves.**

— And what is solid here, and what is yours?

— Solid: the thirty-seven genes and the thirteen proteins; the drawings in the nucleus, and the move that is still going on; the mother's line; the gene of a virus in the placenta; the microbe with not a trace left. Argued about: how the lodger got inside, the two hundred thousand, why exactly thirteen stay at home. Mine: that what we have written is the drawings moving to the head office; that our job today is to want, and that it takes three lines of a man; that the host will learn that job too. And all of it together is a precedent and not a proof.

— One thing I can't get over, — said Oleg. — Ten million billion of them in me alone, and not one says: I am not burning today. And the cells themselves: the liver does not go on strike against the head. With us, three men in a team, and already one is shirking and one is carrying things home. How did they come to terms?

— Some don't. But that is for tomorrow. On your way home look under your feet.

— Ants? They are after my aphids.

— Ask them why they don't go on strike.

Oleg picked up the branch with the lichen, turned it over and put it not on the fire but beside the log.

— Let the lodgers live.

— Well, until tomorrow.

— Bye.
